### IA-01 — Balance users, content, and organizational context

- **Trigger:** Defining navigation, restructuring a content-rich product, or deciding whether a reference app's architecture fits.
- **Author’s account:** IA is the combined organization, labeling, navigation and search of an information environment. Useful architecture emerges from the interaction of people, content and context; content includes services, applications and metadata, not merely prose.
- **Rationale / application:** Before choosing containers, record the relevant audience's information needs and prior knowledge; the content types, granularity, volume, growth, currency and ownership; and the organization's goals, maintenance capacity and channel constraints. Use real representative objects to challenge the structure. Identify the missing axis when a beautiful reference does not fit. Separate an observed constraint from an assumed one.
- **Exception / conflict:** This is a lens for the current decision, not a mandatory discovery phase for every change. User goals remain central, but an architecture whose metadata nobody can maintain will fail. Do not copy a competitor's structure or expose an internal org chart merely because it is convenient for the organization.
- **Review:** Can the proposed grouping be explained in terms of actual user needs, content properties and feasible upkeep? What changes when the collection grows or ownership is distributed?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 1–2, printed pp. 3–38 / PDF25–60; especially printed24–25,31–38 / PDF46–47,53–60. Figure2-6 visually checked at PDF54. Historical product descriptions and statistics are illustrations, not current facts.

### IA-02 — Match finding support and success criteria to the information need

- **Trigger:** Designing a directory, catalog, help center, research tool, recommendations or saved-item experience.
- **Author’s account:** Known-item seeking, exploratory seeking, exhaustive research and refinding are different information needs. A single-query/single-answer model only fits some situations; people may lack the terms for what they need, change their question, or finish partially satisfied.
- **Rationale / application:** Name the need in the scenario before choosing search, categories or a wizard. Known-item work needs a recognizable match; exploration needs meaningful groupings and cues that teach the domain; exhaustive work needs visibility into scope and omissions; refinding needs a dependable route back to something already encountered. Choose success evidence accordingly: speed alone cannot establish that exploration produced useful understanding or that research was complete.
- **Exception / conflict:** These are useful recurring needs, not exhaustive or fixed user types. A single person can switch needs during a session. Do not add every finding feature to a small product, or insist every exploratory journey has one predetermined destination.
- **Review:** What counts as enough information for this scenario? Can someone who lacks the exact term begin, someone with an exact target reach it, and a returning person recover a useful item where those needs are in scope?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, printed39–45 / PDF61–67. The source's population figure and named product features are dated examples, not implementation requirements.

### IA-03 — Support learning across search, browse and asking

- **Trigger:** A finding journey requires refinement, category exploration, related items, or a handoff to human help.
- **Author’s account:** Information seeking combines searching, browsing and asking over multiple iterations. Bates's berry-picking model describes collecting useful pieces while the query evolves; pearl-growing starts with one useful item and seeks related material. Some environments first require finding the relevant collection, then finding within it.
- **Rationale / application:** Connect useful results back to meaningful categories or related items, and make search within an area distinguishable from search across the whole environment. Carry the relevant query, scope, selection or finding context through these transitions when supported. For escalation, show a plausible help route and the context the person will need rather than treating a zero-result page as the end of the task. Check that people can revise their understanding without being forced to restart.
- **Exception / conflict:** A direct factual lookup may need only a short path. A related-item recommendation must have a defensible relationship; neither a decorative carousel nor a speculative AI answer proves that relation. Asking is an architectural option, not permission to contact anyone or promise unavailable support.
- **Review:** Starting from a useful result, can the person refine, broaden, discover related material and return with their context intact? Are the collection and query scope clear at each transition?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, printed46–50 / PDF68–72. Figure3-4 visually checked at PDF69: changing queries, intervening thought, multiple information objects and exit are distinct; it is a conceptual model, not a measured conversion funnel. Named search-engine features are historical.

### IA-04 — Preserve semantic coherence while adapting channels and change rates

- **Trigger:** The same service spans native app, web, support or physical touchpoints, or a redesign changes the product's major sections.
- **Author’s account:** Information environments communicate a sense of place through concepts, language, relationships and ordering. Those structures can persist across different interfaces. Stable semantic layers and more rapidly changing presentation layers need different treatment; modular subenvironments can accommodate growth. The Disneyland website prioritizes trip planning rather than reproducing the park's physical lands as its primary navigation.
- **Rationale / application:** Establish which objects, terms, roles and relationships must remain recognizable across channels; then choose local navigation and emphasis for each channel's actual tasks and constraints. Keep meaningful category boundaries when updating appearance. Test plausible additions against the model before using vague catch-all labels for flexibility. Separate distinct subdomains when their content and rate of change justify it, while retaining clear relationships to the whole.
- **Exception / conflict:** Coherence does not require identical tabs, screen layouts or top-level ordering everywhere. Familiar patterns provide orientation but do not prohibit useful differentiation or expressive design. A metaphor may explain a domain while falsely implying capabilities; preserve its explanatory value without inheriting those false promises. Do not redesign all channels during a narrow requested edit.
- **Review:** Can a person recognize the same object and its meaning across the relevant channels? Does each channel prioritize its own task without unexplained renaming? Can a plausible new content type fit without dissolving the existing categories?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.1, printed16–21 / PDF38–43; ch.4, printed53–75 / PDF75–97, especially59–69,71–74 / PDF81–91,93–96. Figure4-15 and its context visually checked at PDF94; the park comparison is historical and includes an explicitly unknown future park column.

### IA-05 — Design from both the overview and the individual content object

- **Trigger:** Search, notifications, shared links or external links can bring people directly into a detail screen or document.
- **Author’s account:** Top-down IA anticipates broad user questions through a centrally designed overview. Bottom-up IA arises from the content's structure, sequence, metadata and contextual links. Search configuration, controlled vocabulary and editorially selected results also shape experience even when invisible.
- **Rationale / application:** Pair the section map with representative content objects. Define meaningful chunks and their order; identify the fields and relationships needed to browse, filter or navigate onward. Inspect a detail page without arriving through the home screen: it should establish what the item is, where it belongs and useful next destinations. Trace visible finding controls to actual metadata or service support instead of assuming a screenshot proves the underlying capability.
- **Exception / conflict:** Not every detail requires global chrome or every metadata field on screen. Local context can be sufficient; exposing internal implementation is not the objective. A polished homepage cannot compensate for contextless deep entries, but a rich content model does not eliminate a useful overview either.
- **Review:** On direct entry, can someone identify this item's type, context and available onward paths? Do proposed filters and related links have populated fields or relationships behind them? Is missing support recorded as a dependency?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.5, printed79–95 / PDF101–117, especially83–94 / PDF105–116. Photos, Epicurious and BBC examples illustrate architectural mechanisms, not present-day UI contracts.

### IA-06 — Choose organization schemes for the way people seek information

- **Trigger:** Choosing category boundaries, sorting, browsing options, or how to combine unlike content types.
- **Author’s account:** An organization scheme defines the characteristic used to group content; a structure defines relationships among groups and items. Exact schemes such as alphabetic, chronological and geographic arrangements suit predictable lookup. Ambiguous schemes such as topic and task require interpretation but support exploration and associative learning. Different perspectives and heterogeneous formats or granularities make universal classification brittle.
- **Rationale / application:** State the grouping principle before choosing its navigation widget. Match exact access to what the person actually knows and topical/task access to what they need to learn or do. Distinguish content type and granularity where an article, a collection and a tool would otherwise look like interchangeable siblings. Use complementary routes when the same collection supports different needs. Record category inclusion criteria and test borderline objects, rather than treating labels alone as the architecture.
- **Exception / conflict:** An alphabetic list is not necessarily useful to someone who lacks the target name; a topical tree is not automatically better for exact lookup. Geographic boundaries and naming can be contested. Multiple routes add maintenance and should answer real needs, not every conceivable perspective. Internal political compromises are constraints to disclose, not evidence that a grouping serves users.
- **Review:** Can a content owner classify representative and ambiguous items consistently? Can intended users predict where those items belong, including items at different levels of granularity? Is the scheme explained independently of its menu presentation?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, printed97–110,129–131 / PDF119–132,151–153, especially103–109 / PDF125–131. Library examples and claims about relative usage are source accounts, not fresh evidence for this product.

### IA-07 — Keep hybrid and audience routes understandable

- **Trigger:** A menu mixes tasks, topics, audiences, formats or metaphors, or asks people to choose a role before seeing content.
- **Author’s account:** A coherent scheme helps people form a mental model; deeply mixing several schemes makes them inspect each item independently. Multiple schemes can coexist when visually and conceptually separated. Shallow global navigation can legitimately mix major tasks and topics. Audience organization helps when audiences are distinct, but a person may belong to more than one.
- **Rationale / application:** Identify the principle behind each group. Separate topic navigation, high-priority actions and audience entry points when their boundaries would otherwise be unclear. Where audience routes are useful, let people recover from a mistaken role choice or access another role's relevant content unless real access rules forbid it. Keep classification separate from authorization: calling a section 'For researchers' does not make it a security boundary.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not reject a useful global task link merely to achieve taxonomic purity. Role-specific experiences can be appropriate for genuine permissions or subscription differences. The issue is whether the structure remains predictable for actual tasks and overlapping roles, not whether every navigation label has the same grammatical form.
- **Review:** Can someone explain the grouping rule without reading every option? Can a student who is also doing research reach relevant resources without creating a new identity or assuming they are excluded? Is an access restriction backed by actual product policy?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, printed110–116 / PDF132–138, especially112–115 / PDF134–137. Source examples demonstrate tradeoffs; they do not establish measured superiority or authorize changing permissions.

### IA-08 — Combine structures without rigid menu-count or depth rules

- **Trigger:** Designing a large hierarchy, a structured catalog, cross-listing, facets, related links or user tagging.
- **Author’s account:** Hierarchies provide an overview; structured records and metadata enable dynamic filtering, sorting and associations; hypertext and tagging can supply additional relationships. Limited cross-listing can handle ambiguity, but indiscriminate cross-listing weakens a hierarchy. Different facets may legitimately contain the same items. The book rejects using seven-plus-or-minus-two as a navigation-link limit.
- **Rationale / application:** Use a coherent overview where needed, structured attributes for homogeneous collections, and contextual relationships for useful lateral paths. Distinguish 'appears under two parents in this taxonomy' from 'appears in two independent views of the same collection.' Evaluate breadth and depth through label clarity, scanning, grouping, meaningful progress and representative tasks. Validate the data required for proposed filters rather than drawing unsupported controls.
- **Exception / conflict:** Hierarchy is a starting option, not a mandate for every product. Free tags can reveal user language and support social discovery but do not automatically supply consistent classification. The source's later warning about more than two or three levels is a dated heuristic, not a click-count acceptance test; a longer understandable route can outperform a short ambiguous one. Market outcomes of named tagging services are not proof of a general causal claim.
- **Review:** Can users understand the current scope and move to useful alternatives without losing their bearings? Are cross-listings intentional, metadata fields populated, and tag-based recommendations distinguishable from curated structure? Does each level make a meaningful decision easier?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, printed116–131 / PDF138–153, especially118–126 / PDF140–148. Figures6-12 and6-13 visually checked at PDF140–141: diagrams illustrate structure and the breadth/depth tradeoff, not numeric performance thresholds.

### IA-09 — Design a labeling system and check destination promises

- **Trigger:** Renaming navigation, defining a new category set, using icons alone, or finding inconsistent link and page names.
- **Author’s account:** Labels represent larger information chunks. Contextual links, headings, navigation options and index terms have different contexts and consistency needs. A label's meaning depends on neighboring labels, hierarchy, audience and channel; the same word can become ambiguous when responsive navigation removes supporting context. The book's inventory compares navigation labels with destination headings and document titles.
- **Rationale / application:** For consequential naming, inventory current term, intended meaning, actual destination and sibling terms. Reconcile link/menu labels with destination headings and titles so the arrival fulfills the promise, without requiring literal identity. Check parallel syntax, specificity, coverage and terminology within each set. Inspect labels in the actual mobile, desktop or native context, including their surrounding explanation and the accessible name. Use text or another clear cue when an icon's meaning is uncertain.
- **Exception / conflict:** Contextual prose links can legitimately vary; navigation labels need stronger stability because people encounter them repeatedly. Familiar specialist terms may suit a specialist audience. Brand language is acceptable when it remains interpretable; decorative novelty is not evidence of clarity. A justified mixed task/topic global menu need not be rewritten solely for grammatical uniformity.
- **Review:** Before activating an important link, what content or action would the intended audience predict? Does the destination fulfill that expectation? Do the same words represent the same concept and different words distinguish different concepts across relevant states and channels?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.7, printed133–160 / PDF155–182, especially141–148,152–160 / PDF163–170,174–182. Figure7-2 at PDF159 and Table7-1 at PDF180 visually checked. Historical HTML meta-keyword examples are not adopted as current web-search ranking advice.

### IA-10 — Treat candidate labels as hypotheses and maintain the vocabulary

- **Trigger:** Choosing terminology without direct evidence, reconciling novice and specialist language, or expanding a taxonomy.
- **Author’s account:** Existing labels, comparable products, controlled vocabularies, content, authors, user advocates, users and search logs can supply candidates. Open card sorting elicits groupings and category names; closed sorting can examine interpretation of existing categories; free-listing elicits terms. Card sorting removes labels from their normal interface context. Labels require ongoing revision as people and content change.
- **Rationale / application:** Start with working project language and inspect what it represents. Use relevant source evidence to develop candidates; distinguish creator terminology from user terminology. When appropriate, ask people what a label means before asking them to sort content, then test the promising wording in representative task context. Record preferred terms, necessary aliases and scope notes; use realistic near-term content additions to expose gaps. Plan who maintains the vocabulary and what evidence should prompt revision.
- **Exception / conflict:** Competitor prevalence, an author's suggestion and an automated extraction are candidate sources, not proof of comprehension. Search logs reveal expressed queries but not the complete need or success. Do not claim participants or research that did not occur. Do not infer statistical sufficiency from the source's example counts, create hypothetical product areas merely to fill every gap, or normalize away meaningful audience differences.
- **Review:** What evidence supports this label, and what remains a hypothesis? Does it work in the real task as well as a card sort? Can plausible new content be classified without an unexplained 'Miscellaneous' bucket, and is there an owner for future changes?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.7, printed157–173 / PDF179–195, especially164–172 / PDF186–194. Tables7-4–7-6 and their context visually checked at PDF189–190: participant interpretations vary, and sorting is not equivalent to in-context usability evidence. Tool names and numeric examples are historical.

### IA-11 — Orient people who enter anywhere

- **Trigger:** Navigation, deep links, external search entry, notifications, or a content-heavy redesign.
- **Author’s account:** Navigation supplies context and freedom of movement. Global, local, and contextual navigation answer different questions; a hierarchy alone does not support all useful lateral movement. Every destination may become someone's first page.
- **Rationale / application:** Test a meaningful deep destination without first visiting Home: identify the product, current object/section, its relationship to the wider system, and useful next moves. Specify global destinations, nearby destinations, and related content separately, then compose them together so navigation does not overwhelm the content. Preserve browser/native navigation expectations. Treat repeated local differences as intentional only when content or user needs justify them.
- **Exception / conflict:** These are semantic roles, not a mandate for three visible bars or desktop navigation on iPhone. A small app may satisfy several roles with one control. Do not force users through ancestors merely because the storage hierarchy has them. Conventional placement and icon examples from 2015 do not set current platform rules.
- **Review:** From a direct link, can a person explain where they are, distinguish their next destinations, reach relevant related content, and return predictably? Is the orientation claim based on an actual traversal or only a static inspection?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.8, PDF197–215 / printed175–193, especially PDF202–205 and210–214. Visually inspected PDF199, Figures8-1/8-2: the global/local/contextual roles persist across desktop/mobile while their layouts change.

### IA-12 — Offer complementary routes without making personalization the only map

- **Trigger:** A large collection, mixed user expertise, guided setup, or personalized discovery.
- **Author’s account:** Sitemaps communicate hierarchy; alphabetical indexes support known terms without requiring hierarchy knowledge; guides support a chosen task or audience. Personalization infers preferences while customization asks users to configure them. Both depend on a useful underlying structure and neither resolves every finding problem.
- **Rationale / application:** Choose supplemental routes for actual information needs: a broad overview, term-based lookup, or a guided decision. Keep guides escapable and allow correction/revisit where task dependencies permit. Maintain a dependable route to the collection outside recommendations. Make the effect of configuration choices understandable, including consequential downstream changes.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not require a sitemap, A–Z index, and tutorial in every product. Tiny environments may need none. Expert or repeated workflows can be burdened by guidance. Recommendations based on incomplete history can be wrong; social popularity can narrow discovery. A user-controlled favorite is different from an inferred recommendation.
- **Review:** If a recommendation is irrelevant or a person does not share the assumed vocabulary, can they still reach the needed item? Can they exit a guide and revise a configuration without losing useful context?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.8, PDF215–231 / printed193–209. Figure8-2 at PDF199 inspected. Historical social-network behavior and predictions are examples, not present-day algorithm claims.

### IA-13 — Decide whether search can be supported, not just whether it can be drawn

- **Trigger:** Adding search, redesigning a search-heavy product, or proposing search to fix poor navigation.
- **Author’s account:** Search depends on content, indexing, query processing, result presentation, and continuing configuration. There is no universal item-count threshold for needing it. Information need, collection size/dynamism, fragmentation, user expectations, and maintenance capacity matter.
- **Rationale / application:** Establish the search job and alternatives before adding a box. Record the corpus, supported fields and query behavior, result actions, and maintenance owner/capability. Separate failures caused by missing content, indexing, language mismatch, ranking, or presentation. Use available query evidence to refine both search and the wider architecture; do not invent logs. Identify backend dependencies explicitly.
- **Exception / conflict:** Improving navigation is normally valuable, but search may be a practical interim route through fragmented content when structural repair is unavailable. Small, highly specific collections can still merit search. The book's suggested third-party products, algorithm counts, and historical expectations are not current selection guidance.
- **Review:** For a representative query, can the team trace the intended item from source content through index and matching to an actionable result? Is a mock search distinguished from an implemented and exercised capability?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.9, PDF233–240 / printed211–218. PDF239, Figure9-1, visually inspected: the diagram includes creators/content as well as engine/interface/users, supporting end-to-end diagnosis.

### IA-14 — Define the searchable unit and make scope legible

- **Trigger:** Mixed content types, fielded search, results polluted by navigation/boilerplate, or scoped search.
- **Author’s account:** Indexing everything is not necessarily useful. Search zones select coherent subsets; document components permit more precise matching and more useful result displays. Narrowing improves focus but introduces complexity and can hide relevant content.
- **Rationale / application:** Decide which entities and fields are searched, which content is excluded or downweighted, and why. Align zones with user-understandable distinctions. Show the active scope and a route to broaden it. Distinguish a section search from a global search even if the visual control is shared. Use realistic queries to check whether repetitive navigation, legal text, or incidental mentions crowd out useful destinations.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book's navigation-versus-destination distinction is contextual, not a rule to exclude every category page. An overview can be the intended destination. Reviews and discussions may be valuable searchable content for some tasks despite the book's restaurant example. Do not hide useful items solely to obtain a smaller count.
- **Review:** Can someone tell what was searched and intentionally change the scope? Do test queries retrieve the intended entity/field, including a relevant item outside the current zone after broadening?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.9, PDF240–249 / printed218–227 and PDF282–286 / printed260–264. Example reductions in result count are illustrative, not proof of improved relevance.

### IA-15 — Match retrieval tradeoffs to the cost of misses and noise

- **Trigger:** Search ranking, query expansion, synonyms, spelling correction, or evaluation of retrieval quality.
- **Author’s account:** Precision concerns the relevant portion of retrieved results; recall concerns how much of the relevant collection was retrieved. Stemming, synonyms, fields, and other query transformations affect retrieval. Some tasks need a few strong answers; others need broad coverage.
- **Rationale / application:** State whether missed relevant items or irrelevant results are more costly for this task. Use representative queries and a judged example set to evaluate changes; keep exact matches recognizable when expanding terms. Distinguish correcting a likely typo, matching an alias, including related concepts, and broadening to narrower terms. Explain or offer reversal of a transformation when it materially changes meaning.
- **Exception / conflict:** Precision and recall often trade off under a particular change; they are not universally, mathematically inverse. Ch.9's absolute phrasing is stronger than Ch.10's own qualification. Do not claim corpus-level recall without knowing or estimating the relevant corpus. Existing engine behavior and evidence constrain promises; semantic or AI search still needs evaluation rather than an assumed guarantee.
- **Review:** Which relevant items were missed, which irrelevant items were added, and why? Does a model/SKU/name query retain its intended specificity after normalization or expansion?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.9, PDF249–255 / printed227–233; ch.10, PDF293–297 / printed271–275. PDF250, Figure9-7, visually inspected to verify distinct denominators. The old Bellcore result is not a performance promise for this product.

### IA-16 — Make results support recognition, judgment, and the next action

- **Trigger:** Search results, resource listings, comparisons, or result ranking/sorting controls.
- **Author’s account:** Result content depends on information need and available structure. Known-item finding benefits from distinguishing fields; exploratory finding often needs descriptive context. Sorting and ranking serve different purposes, and result usefulness extends to selecting, acting, or saving.
- **Rationale / application:** Choose fields that distinguish realistic near-duplicates and support the decision: version, date, owner, location, availability, or relevant excerpt as appropriate. Offer task-relevant ordering and name its meaning. Let someone act directly when the visible information supports the action; preserve necessary decision detail. Separate saving current selected items from saving a query that may yield different items later.
- **Exception / conflict:** More metadata can bury useful results; do not show all available fields. Popularity, expert recommendations, personal relevance, and paid placement are different signals. Do not imply payment proves quality or relevance, despite the book's favorable PFP speculation. Distinguish commercial promotion from ranking rationale. Historical “best bets” need real editorial ownership, not invented recommendations.
- **Review:** Can a person distinguish similar results and explain the active order? Can they complete the intended next action without opening every result, while retaining essential consequences and provenance?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.9, PDF255–274 / printed233–252, especially PDF256–258,262,266–273. The source's cited grouping study was not independently reviewed; no measured improvement is claimed here.

### IA-17 — Treat search as an iterative finding loop with recovery

- **Trigger:** Search refinement, zero/many results, autocomplete, advanced search, or transitions between browse and search.
- **Author’s account:** Queries evolve as users learn. A simple initial interface can reveal refinement at the point of need. Search and browsing should connect. Results should restate relevant query/scope/settings, and zero results should provide a useful next route.
- **Rationale / application:** Preserve editable query, active constraints, and meaningful result context. Support narrowing excessive results and broadening empty ones. Connect result categories and item metadata to browsing; make any change of scope explicit. Offer useful recovery such as correcting terms, removing a filter, browsing a relevant category, or an actual available support route. Evaluate the transition from result to detail and back, not only the first result screen.
- **Exception / conflict:** A single search field is a default, not a ban on well-labeled structured queries such as origin/destination. Advanced search can be the main workbench for expert tasks. Do not expose engine syntax merely to show technical detail. Autocomplete must not promise an unavailable result, and empty-state suggestions must not disguise an outage as “no matches.” Distinguishing transport/index failures is this skill's operational extension.
- **Review:** With a misspelling, restrictive filter, empty result, and return from a detail view, can the person revise the search without reconstructing it? Do controls describe their actual scope and supported behavior?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.9, PDF274–289 / printed252–267. Source discusses result revision; state restoration and failure-category checks translate that reasoning into implementation criteria.

### IA-18 — Accept users' vocabulary while maintaining stable concepts

- **Trigger:** Conflicting labels, synonyms, aliases, acronyms, localized names, misspellings, or content tagging.
- **Author’s account:** Synonym rings connect terms for retrieval; authority files select preferred terms; taxonomies organize concepts; thesauri add equivalence, hierarchy, and association. These structures can coordinate authoring, navigation, indexes, and search.
- **Rationale / application:** Define a canonical concept and display label with supported aliases, scope, and evidence from content/users. Keep authoring and visible labels consistent while accepting useful entry terms. Maintain concept identity independently of label spelling when building a data model. Distinguish an exact alias from a broader concept or merely related product; choose the smallest vocabulary mechanism that solves the actual problem.
- **Exception / conflict:** Retrieval equivalence is context-dependent, not a claim that objects are interchangeable. The book groups competing devices and category names as equivalents in examples; do not silently replace a requested competitor/model with your own product. Related alternatives can be offered with explicit differences. A preferred term must not require users to know internal jargon.
- **Review:** Do actual alternate terms reach the intended concept? Are broad or related matches visibly distinguishable from exact matches, and is a label change possible without breaking references?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.10, PDF291–307 / printed269–285; PDF317–322 / printed295–300. PDF305, Figure10-11, inspected. Stable identity separate from labels is an implementation recommendation, not a quoted book schema.

### IA-19 — Choose vocabulary architecture according to what can actually be changed

- **Trigger:** Introducing a thesaurus, improving a legacy search system, or integrating external/dynamic content.
- **Author’s account:** A classic thesaurus is applied at indexing and searching; an indexing thesaurus controls content tags without query mapping; a searching thesaurus transforms queries without requiring document-level indexing. Each makes different promises and carries different costs.
- **Rationale / application:** Inspect control over content ingestion, tagging, search processing, and presentation. If only tags are controllable, use consistent browsable groupings and record the missing alias support. If content cannot be manually indexed, evaluate a query-side vocabulary against the real corpus. Plan ownership, maintenance, and a migration path proportionate to the collection; do not propose a full thesaurus by default.
- **Exception / conflict:** An indexing-only vocabulary does not automatically understand a user's variants. Query expansion without content tagging can increase noise and depends strongly on mapping quality. A vendor's claim of standards support does not establish compatible APIs, storage formats, or correct behavior. Check current standards only when implementing an actual compliance requirement.
- **Review:** Where is vocabulary applied, what remains unsupported, and can a representative alias be traced through the implemented path? Is the maintenance workload realistic for the people and systems available?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.10, PDF307–317 / printed285–295. PDF312, Figure10-19, visually inspected to verify the indexing/searching axes. Book-era PubMed behavior and named standards are historical examples, not current claims.

### IA-20 — Model different semantic relationships explicitly

- **Trigger:** Building a taxonomy, related-content network, terminology registry, or shared metadata model.
- **Author’s account:** Equivalence, broader/narrower hierarchy, and association serve different retrieval purposes. Hierarchy itself may describe class membership, part/whole, or instances. Preferred terms, qualifiers, and scope notes constrain ambiguity; term selection balances content vocabulary and users' language.
- **Rationale / application:** Record relationship type and intended use instead of using one generic “related” relation for everything. Disambiguate homographs and write scope notes for indexers when needed. Check that narrower terms actually satisfy the selected relationship; keep associated products or causes outside false parent-child structures. Choose term granularity to match the collection and retrieval task, not grammatical tidiness alone.
- **Exception / conflict:** No universal noun-only, plural-only, or compound-term rule fits every context. Task navigation may need verbs; one collection may need a compound term that another can express with independent facets. Do not infer logical inheritance across every parent-child relationship or treat contextual association as equivalence.
- **Review:** Can authors consistently classify a realistic ambiguous item? Are parent, alias, and related-item links distinguishable, reciprocal where intended, and useful for the specific finding task?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.10, PDF304–307 / printed282–285 and PDF317–323 / printed295–301. Table10-2's language/cognition generalization is treated as a contextual guideline, not an independently established universal fact.

### IA-21 — Allow multiple routes to one item without multiplying the item

- **Trigger:** Content belongs in multiple categories, cross-listing, breadcrumbs, or inconsistent detail destinations.
- **Author’s account:** Polyhierarchy allows a term to appear under multiple broader concepts. Digital content avoids the physical one-shelf limitation, but the interface must still represent navigational context intelligibly.
- **Rationale / application:** Cross-list a concept when multiple placements reflect genuine user expectations. Preserve one underlying item identity and define the visible hierarchy/context for each route. Distinguish a semantic parent from the path used to arrive; make Back and any breadcrumb or parent link behave coherently. Check linking and selected category state when the same item is reached through different routes.
- **Exception / conflict:** Cross-listing every item everywhere creates noise and maintenance burden. Polyhierarchy is not equivalent to faceted filtering: the first permits multiple parents, while facets describe independent dimensions. The source says navigation context is the only real challenge; implementation also requires deduplication and maintenance discipline, which are operational extensions here.
- **Review:** Do two category routes reach the same item and consistent facts? Can the person return to their original context without confusing that context with the object's only possible parent?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.10, PDF323–326 / printed301–304. PDF324, Figures10-23/10-24, visually inspected: shared child nodes rather than duplicate objects.

### IA-22 — Build facets from independent descriptive dimensions and usable metadata

- **Trigger:** Filterable catalogs, document libraries, discovery, or a taxonomy mixing subject, audience, format, and location.
- **Author’s account:** Faceted classification describes an item through several dimensions instead of forcing all distinctions into one tree. Each facet may be a flat list or hierarchy. A shared descriptive foundation can support browsing, querying, sorting, and varied interfaces over time.
- **Rationale / application:** Separate dimensions such as topic, audience, format, and region; choose those that meaningfully distinguish real items and user decisions. Specify values, assignment rules, missing-value behavior, and who maintains metadata. Decide which facets to expose at which stage. For implementation, define whether selections combine with AND or OR, dependent facet behavior, removal/reset, counts, and no-match recovery according to actual engine capabilities.
- **Exception / conflict:** A filter panel cannot compensate for absent or unreliable metadata. Do not automatically expose every field or force the book's historical universal facets. Facet independence concerns principles of division, not a guarantee that values are statistically independent. Selection semantics and missing-value handling are this skill's build criteria rather than explicit book rules.
- **Review:** Can representative items be described consistently, including one with missing metadata? Does a user reach the same result set through equivalent browse/search combinations, understand active constraints, and undo an overrestrictive combination?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.10, PDF325–331 / printed303–309. PDF326, Figure10-26, visually inspected; text and captions on PDF327–329 distinguish flat/hierarchical facets and reuse in search/sort. Historical Wine.com interfaces illustrate possibilities, not current requirements.

### IA-23 — Plan research around the missing architectural evidence

- **Trigger:** Planning research for a major redesign with uncertain data, ownership, technical capacity, or user evidence.
- **Author’s account:** IA research balances business and technical context, the actual content collection, and people’s needs and information-seeking behavior. The research, strategy, design, implementation, and administration activities overlap and recur; they are not a rigid waterfall. Strategy begins to form during research and changes as evidence arrives.
- **Rationale / application:** Inspect existing goals, constraints, system behavior, content ownership, and relevant user evidence. Identify which sources can answer each uncertainty. Compare the promised experience with available content, search/indexing capabilities, contributor capacity, and maintenance resources. Keep a compact context/content/users gap record; convert each consequential gap into a decision, dependency, or named unknown. Preserve useful existing architecture rather than assuming redesign means starting over.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not require interviews, kickoff meetings, all research methods, or a long report for every change. The source’s face-to-face imperative and meeting-size examples are historical working preferences. Existing authorization and access boundaries govern contact with stakeholders. Business goals constrain the design but do not justify inventing user needs.
- **Review:** Does each consequential architectural choice fit the actual user task, content characteristics, and operational capacity? What would fail if an assumed capability or content owner did not exist?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 11, printed pp. 313–325 (PDF 335–347), 349–353 (371–375); ch. 12, pp. 355–361 (377–383). Figure 11-2/11-3 visually checked at PDF 338. The phase model is a planning framework, not a mandatory sequence.

### IA-24 — Study representative content before committing to categories or fields

- **Trigger:** Designing a catalog, library, knowledge base, search/filter system, or migration without a trustworthy understanding of its content.
- **Author’s account:** Content analysis reveals the structure, vocabulary, metadata, and relationships hidden in real objects. A useful sample covers different formats, document types, sources, subjects, and other consequential dimensions. High-value rare items matter alongside common ones. Early surveys and later inventories/audits have different purposes.
- **Rationale / application:** Sample the actual collection for variety, importance, and edge cases; do not merely walk the current navigation and reproduce its blind spots. Examine structural metadata (parts and independently useful chunks), descriptive metadata (subject/type/audience), and administrative metadata (ownership and lifecycle). Record missing or inconsistent fields and compare the sample with proposed categories and templates. Revisit objects when patterns emerge; distinguish a content inventory’s locations from an audit’s quality, gaps, and required actions.
- **Exception / conflict:** A sample is not a complete migration inventory. Stop exploratory sampling when new examples cease to change the model, while stating its limits; use a complete accounting where the task requires migrating every item. Do not mechanically exclude rare content or impose the book’s suggested sample quantities. A pretty fixture set does not establish that production metadata supports facets.
- **Review:** Can the proposed structure accommodate representative, unusually shaped, and important rare content? Which required fields are actually populated, and what will happen to objects that do not fit?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 11, printed pp. 325–330 (PDF 347–352); ch. 13, pp. 414–420 (436–442). This adapts qualitative sampling into build checks; it does not claim a statistically representative sample.

### IA-25 — Diagnose findability using search behavior and support evidence

- **Trigger:** Users cannot find content, labels are disputed, queries return zero or excessive results, or analytics are being used to justify navigation changes.
- **Author’s account:** Search logs expose the terms people actually use and the information they seek. Zero-hit queries, overly broad results, changing query demand, and post-query behavior can guide vocabulary, labels, content, and search improvements. Support staff and support records reveal recurring questions. Clickstream data alone often cannot explain intent or success.
- **Rationale / application:** Where accessible and relevant, inspect actual queries and outcomes, failed searches, common reformulations, and support questions. For an observed failure, distinguish absent content, unindexed content, vocabulary mismatch, scope/filter restrictions, and poor ranking or presentation before choosing a fix. Feed real variant terms into labeling and retrieval decisions. Connect patterns to a reproducible query/task and inspect the current result instead of treating volume as proof of success.
- **Exception / conflict:** Logs reflect the current product and observed population, not all potential needs. Popularity, dwell time, or departure alone do not establish usefulness, frustration, or causation. The source’s analytics product capabilities, tracking suggestions, and typical query-length examples are dated and are not an instruction to collect personal data or adopt surveillance. Missing logs stay an evidence gap.
- **Review:** Can the proposed change explain and improve a specific findability failure? Is it repairing the actual cause, and have unchanged cases been considered?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 11, printed pp. 334–338 (PDF 356–360), with benchmarking limits at pp. 330–333 (352–355). The diagnostic categories above are the skill’s implementation synthesis of these observations and the book’s search model.

### IA-26 — Choose an IA test for the uncertainty it can resolve

- **Trigger:** Choosing category boundaries or labels, validating a new hierarchy, or claiming that a navigation structure is understandable.
- **Author’s account:** Open card sorts elicit users’ groupings and vocabulary; closed sorts examine a proposed set of categories. Card wording, granularity, heterogeneous items, cross-listing, and participant selection influence the findings. Task performance can examine known-item and exhaustive needs, topic and action tasks, novices and experienced users. Stripped-down hierarchy tests reduce visual distractions but never fully remove interface effects.
- **Rationale / application:** Use open sorting for discovery, closed sorting for placement ambiguity, and task-based hierarchy/wireframe tests for finding actual destinations. Define the question first, select realistic items and neutral task wording, and capture reasoning and errors as well as completion. Use some content not used to construct the hierarchy to expose overfitting. Inspect categories in context too: ordering, sample subcategories, and visible affordances change information scent. Record participants, procedure, artifacts, and limitations when tests are actually run.
- **Exception / conflict:** A successful closed sort does not demonstrate successful navigation, and a successful stripped-down hierarchy test does not establish the finished UI’s usability. Qualitative cluster diagrams and small-sample percentages are not statistical proof. Focus groups can generate ideas but do not substitute for individual task performance. An agent inspection or synthetic sort is not a participant study; do not fabricate sessions or metrics. The source’s card counts, timing, and informal-friend examples are not universal research standards.
- **Review:** Does the evidence method fit the claim? Can intended users find the destination under realistic wording, ambiguity, and entry conditions, or is that still a hypothesis?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 11, printed pp. 338–349 (PDF 360–371); ch. 12, pp. 364–366 (386–388). Figures 11-9/11-10 visually checked at PDF 369: the simplified model retains a boundary-spanning item, rather than forcing every item into one disjoint cluster.

### IA-27 — Turn research into a feasible architectural strategy

- **Trigger:** Multiple plausible structures, substantial redesign, or research findings that have not become concrete implementation decisions.
- **Author’s account:** IA strategy bridges research and design by reconciling top-down organization and labeling with bottom-up object types and metadata. It also covers navigation, technology integration, and administration. Research and strategy co-evolve through thinking, articulating, communicating, and testing. Incomplete content or business plans need not halt the work.
- **Rationale / application:** Recommend a coherent architecture with explicit scope, dominant organization approach, important alternate access paths, content types/metadata, and dependencies. Trace observation → interpretation → decision → expected behavior, distinguishing evidence from inference. Test the proposal against representative tasks and content, then expose contradictions. Separate immediately feasible improvements from work requiring indexing, content cleanup, ownership, or infrastructure. Record who or what must supply each dependency without inventing agreement.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not force a 50-page report, phase gate, or stakeholder presentation into a small task. The book’s 1999 Weather.com case illustrates a reasoning chain; its personalization, anonymous tracking, return-traffic objectives, and platform assumptions are not present-day recommendations. A screenshot or competitor example is not proof of a strategy’s outcomes.
- **Review:** Can another contributor tell what changes, why, what supports the choice, and what must be built or maintained to make it real? Is a major unresolved assumption visible rather than hidden behind a diagram?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 12, printed pp. 355–366 (PDF 377–388), 373–388 (395–410). Table 12-1 visually checked at PDF 400; retained its traceability structure, not its historical conclusions.

### IA-28 — Use different artifacts for structure, behavior, and semantics

- **Trigger:** A sitemap is being treated as a complete specification, a wireframe is mistaken for final visual design, or a team cannot interpret a complex architecture diagram.
- **Author’s account:** No single two-dimensional diagram captures a multidimensional information environment. Sitemaps describe components and relationships; wireframes show how architecture meets constrained screen space; content models and vocabularies communicate semantics that diagrams often miss. Views should serve specific audiences and decisions.
- **Rationale / application:** Produce the smallest useful set of views: a hierarchy or relationship map for organization, a wireflow for actions and responses, a representative wireframe for visible navigation/content priorities, and a schema or term table when semantics matter. Label what arrows mean, separate pages from content chunks and groups, and identify intentional omissions. Use consistent component IDs across related artifacts. Add notes for behavior and assumptions that the drawing cannot express; revise the architecture when a constrained layout reveals a real problem.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not generate every possible artifact or wireframe every repeated page. Text outlines or tables are acceptable when clearer. A wireframe is neither behavior validation nor the final visual direction; its typography and spacing are provisional unless deliberately specified. The book’s tool and printable-page conventions are dated. Its recommendation to present diagrams does not authorize contacting others.
- **Review:** Can the intended implementer distinguish a container, content object, link, and state transition? Are consequential relationships and omissions clear, and do the views agree?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 13, printed pp. 389–414 (PDF 411–436), 431–434 (453–456). Figure 13-7 visually checked at PDF 424: separate notation for page, component, group, and directional link. Reconcile with this skill’s existing flow and prototype-fidelity guidance rather than duplicate it.

### IA-29 — Map content to destinations without confusing content with containers

- **Trigger:** Content migration, splitting or combining pages, reusing information across channels, or translating a legacy structure into a new architecture.
- **Author’s account:** A content chunk is the smallest portion that merits independent treatment, not automatically a paragraph or page. Source and destination containers need not map one-to-one. Unique identifiers and a mapping table connect the inventory to production; separating content from containers enables controlled reuse.
- **Rationale / application:** Identify chunks by independent retrieval, indexing, reuse, and maintenance needs. Record source identity/location, destination object or template, transformation needed, and any gap. Keep stable identity across inventory, diagrams, and implementation. For reused information, identify the authoritative source and which destinations depend on it. Reconcile old and new coverage so no required content disappears merely because it lacked a convenient page in the new layout.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not atomize every sentence or create a complex content system for a handful of static items. The book’s hierarchy-based IDs illustrate traceability, not a requirement to encode changeable hierarchy into permanent IDs. Preserve approved scope and dependencies; a mapping proposal does not authorize deleting or publishing content. Redirect and access-policy checks may be necessary implementation additions, but are not asserted as this passage’s explicit prescription.
- **Review:** Can each required source item be traced to its intended destination or a documented disposition? Does updating reused content reach the correct places without creating contradictory copies?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 13, printed pp. 403–405 (PDF 425–427), 414–420 (436–442). Figure 13-17 visually checked at PDF 440: distinct source and destination identifiers demonstrate mapping, not page equivalence.

### IA-30 — Make contextual navigation depend on explicit content relationships

- **Trigger:** Related content, product-to-review links, cross-sell, deep-linked detail pages, or a large collection that currently makes users restart at the hierarchy.
- **Author’s account:** Content models connect repeated object types and support horizontal navigation deep within a product. High-volume, homogeneous, valuable content is a strong candidate. Links can be generated from shared metadata; nonunique names can connect the wrong objects, so identifiers or additional attributes are necessary. Modeling reveals missing objects and the metadata worth investing in.
- **Rationale / application:** Define object types, useful directional relationships, entry points, and required linking fields. Give each contextual link a task reason and an implementable matching rule. Prefer stable identity when available; distinguish similarly named entities. Check real metadata completeness and define behavior for missing, ambiguous, stale, or unavailable relationships. Test a deep entry: can someone find the relevant next object without guessing that it exists or returning to a global menu?
- **Exception / conflict:** Manual curated links can suit small collections. Do not imply behavior-based recommendations exist without the necessary data or infer that “related” always means desirable. Popularity-based cross-sell is only one possible relationship. The book’s illustrative compound matching rule is not a guarantee of unique identity. Empty related sections or invented metadata must not conceal unavailable capability.
- **Review:** For each visible relationship, can the system name its source, matching rule, and target? Does a duplicate-title case link to the right entity, and does missing data degrade honestly?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 13, printed pp. 421–428 (PDF 443–450). Figure 13-22 checked at PDF 448 and linking table/rules checked at PDF 449. The figure explicitly includes missing objects; drawing a relationship does not mean its content exists.

### IA-31 — Budget metadata and vocabularies as ongoing product capabilities

- **Trigger:** A proposal adds tags, facets, synonyms, cross-links, or fields without specifying how they will be populated and maintained.
- **Author’s account:** A metadata matrix weighs each vocabulary’s user value against development and maintenance cost. A separate management system holds terms and relationships. Simple preferred/variant lists and sophisticated thesauri require different tooling. Modeling links helps prioritize the fields that actually make an architecture work.
- **Rationale / application:** For each candidate field or vocabulary, name the user task it supports, applicable object types, values/relationships, existing coverage, source, and maintainer. Prioritize fields that drive valuable retrieval or navigation before collecting every plausible attribute. Decide whether simple lists, a structured database, or relationship-aware vocabulary management are justified. State dependencies when the current CMS or index cannot represent the required data.
- **Exception / conflict:** The source’s sample maintenance ratings are contextual, not universal. Geographic, audience, and product vocabularies may be complex in another environment. Historical Word and LAN workflows are not implementation requirements. Shared/global fields and local/type-specific fields can coexist; centralized uniformity is not automatically best.
- **Review:** Could the proposed filters and links be populated accurately now and maintained as content changes? Which field gives enough user value to justify its authoring and governance cost?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 12, printed pp. 356–358 (PDF 378–380), 382–385 (404–407); ch. 13, pp. 427–431 (449–453). Table 13-3 visually checked at PDF 451: the maintenance column varies by vocabulary rather than treating metadata as free.

### IA-32 — Preserve the architectural rationale and maintain it through growth

- **Trigger:** Content grows after launch, teams add new categories or terms, an organization changes, or a redesign risks discarding working structure.
- **Author’s account:** Information architectures keep changing with content, users, and context. An IA style guide records the audience, purposes, content policy, underlying rationale, standards, guidelines, and maintenance procedures. It explains how to extend the system and can be integrated into existing guidance rather than becoming another isolated document.
- **Rationale / application:** Add relevant IA decisions to the project’s existing design context/decisions: what belongs, how labels and relationships are chosen, when categories or terms may be added, who maintains them, and how new/expired content enters or leaves retrieval. Distinguish binding project rules from contextual recommendations. Record the reason and evidence behind important choices, not just the final menu. Recheck the architecture when observed content or behavior invalidates its assumptions; track build-time changes back into the maintained model.
- **Exception / conflict:** No architecture is permanently correct, but growth is not automatic evidence for wholesale redesign. Do not invent ownership or schedule recurring monitoring without authorization. Documentation effort should fit the product; a small project may need only a few decision entries. Source examples such as mouseover-dependent guidance are historical, not current accessibility requirements.
- **Review:** Could a new contributor add, rename, relate, index, or retire an object without silently breaking findability? Are approved rules, agent defaults, and unresolved assumptions distinguishable?
- **Source / dating:** Ch. 11, printed pp. 314–315 (PDF 336–337); ch. 13, pp. 434–439 (456–461); Coda, pp. 441–445 (463–467). Coda reinforces coherent cross-channel finding and understanding; its social-platform growth statistics are historical and not reused.
