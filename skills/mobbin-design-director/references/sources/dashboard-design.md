### DD-01 — Define the monitoring decision before choosing dashboard furniture

- **Trigger:** Designing a dashboard, KPI overview, status screen, or a request to make a product look like an executive dashboard.
- **Author’s account:** Few defines a dashboard around a concentrated overview of the information needed for a person's objectives, enabling monitoring at a glance. It is a presentation medium, not a particular technology, business audience, KPI list, or collection of gauges. Quantitative measures are common, but tasks, issues, schedules, people and relationships can be relevant too.
- **Rationale / application:** Name who monitors what, which condition they must recognize, and what decision or action follows. Select information for that job before choosing cards or chart types. Keep an overview focused on detecting what deserves attention, with a useful path to the details needed for response. Distinguish the monitoring surface from the larger product's analytical, reporting, or operational workspaces. An issue list can be more useful than another numeric tile.
- **Exception / conflict:** The author's definition is an explicitly chosen useful boundary, not a reason to reject the user's product because it calls a scrollable analytics workspace a dashboard. Do not insist on graphics when concise text or a table communicates better. Historical vendor examples illustrate approaches rather than endorse products or establish current capabilities. Role-based customization means fitting the information need; it does not automatically require user-configurable widgets.
- **Review:** Can each prominent item be connected to a stated monitoring objective and a useful next decision? Does the overview expose important conditions without pretending to contain every detail needed for diagnosis or action?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.1, sections1.1–1.4, PDF11–28, especially26–27; ch.2, section2.2.2, PDF36. Figure1-12 visually checked at PDF25: a tabular overview can serve the dashboard role without literal cockpit instruments. 2006 technology, adoption and market claims are historical.

### DD-02 — Match cadence, detail, and interaction to the monitoring role

- **Trigger:** Choosing dashboard refresh behavior, alerting, detail level, historical context, or investigation controls.
- **Author’s account:** Strategic monitoring emphasizes broad performance and direction; analytical work needs richer comparisons, history and routes into investigation; operational monitoring concerns changing events that may need immediate response. Real-time updating is necessary only when the objective requires it. One person may have several monitoring purposes.
- **Rationale / application:** Derive the update interval and permissible data age from the decision's time sensitivity. Use stable periods or snapshots for comparisons that would otherwise move while being examined; expose current event detail when responding to a specific operational problem. Name the actual entity, status and next route required to act. Show the data's period and freshness separately from when the screen last rendered. Check delayed, partial and unavailable data rather than treating a refreshing animation as proof of current information.
- **Exception / conflict:** The three roles are a useful lens, not exhaustive user types or mutually exclusive products. Do not inherit the source's dated assumptions that executives avoid computers or rarely analyze data. Strategic work can need interaction and analysts can investigate live data. Book examples of hover-to-detail do not justify hover-only access on touch or keyboard interfaces. Freshness/error-state checks are this skill's operational extension of the cadence argument.
- **Review:** Does the refresh/detail contract fit the time available to make the decision? Can someone distinguish a current condition, a historical snapshot, and stale or unavailable data, and reach the appropriate investigation or response without losing scope?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.1, section1.3.1, PDF27; ch.2, sections2.1–2.2.1.1, PDF29–35. Role and update-frequency classifications are contextual; neither universal refresh intervals nor present-day product capabilities are supplied by the book.

### DD-03 — Keep decision-critical comparisons visible together

- **Trigger:** Splitting a dashboard into tabs, a carousel, per-item views, nested scroll regions, or a dense mobile overview.
- **Author’s account:** Seeing related information together enables comparison and pattern recognition without relying on memory. Fragmentation is harmful when the information must be compared to fulfill the objective. Navigation to detail or a different independently useful set is explicitly acceptable; the problem is hiding information needed for the same overview decision.
- **Rationale / application:** Identify the comparisons that need concurrent visibility before choosing containers. Keep their values, context and labels close enough to compare, and avoid cycling through one entity at a time when cross-entity judgment is the task. Preserve a compact overview with useful summaries and relevant exceptions; move genuine detail to a clear destination. On narrow layouts, prioritize complete comparison groups and use a dedicated comparison view or controlled reflow when needed.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book's strict single-screen definition reflects its monitoring focus. Do not shrink text, suppress necessary context, disable zoom, or violate native text-scaling/reflow behavior to satisfy a no-scroll rule. Scrolling and multiple views are legitimate for other purposes and accessibility configurations. Describe any loss of simultaneous comparison and compensate for it; this responsive/accessibility adaptation is the skill's judgment, not a claim that the 2006 book prescribed mobile behavior.
- **Review:** Can the intended comparison be made from the currently visible content without remembering hidden values? At supported narrow widths and enlarged text, are decision groups still understandable and critical conditions reachable without clipped or illegible content?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.1, section1.3.1, PDF26–27; ch.3, section3.1, PDF39–43. Figure3-3 visually checked at PDF43: both overall and internal scrolling can conceal information. The source's predictions about users ignoring or resenting offscreen content are contextual assertions, not measured findings for this product.

### DD-04 — Give each metric enough context to support an interpretation

- **Trigger:** KPI tiles, status indicators, targets, current-versus-prior comparisons, or an unlabeled gauge that looks informative.
- **Author’s account:** A current number alone often leaves its meaning unclear. Relevant history, targets, forecasts, norms or related measures can supply context; several comparisons can be useful if primary meaning remains prominent. A good/bad marker without magnitude or reference information can hide how significant the condition is. A quantitative scale without values only suggests information that is absent.
- **Rationale / application:** Establish the metric definition, unit, population/scope, aggregation and period, then choose the comparison that answers the actual question. Label actuals, targets and forecasts distinctly. Compare like periods and compatible definitions, and make mismatches explicit. Show status against a justified threshold rather than inventing good/bad bands. Keep the main finding easy to scan while making its relevant basis available. Treat unavailable values or unknown targets as unknown, not zero or an acceptable status.
- **Exception / conflict:** More comparison data is not automatically better; omit references that do not change the decision. A target may be aspirational while a historical norm is descriptive. An annual target beside month-to-date performance is not inherently invalid, but must not imply a same-period performance comparison. Metric ownership, denominator validation, missing-state handling and data provenance are implementation checks extending the source's context principle. A screenshot cannot verify the underlying calculations or freshness.
- **Review:** Can someone tell what this value measures, over which period and population, relative to what, and why its status was assigned? Do missing, partial, stale and incompatible comparisons remain visibly distinct from measured success or failure?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.2, sections2.2.1.1–2.2.1.3, PDF34–36; ch.3, section3.2, PDF43–45. Table2-3 checked at PDF35 and Figures3-4/3-5 at PDF43–44. The praised call-duration gauge supplies named comparisons; that praise is not blanket endorsement of its gauge geometry or of shorter calls as a universal business goal.

### DD-05 — Use a direct measure and precision appropriate to the decision

- **Trigger:** Metric calculation, denominator or percentage-change questions; a dashboard makes users calculate a variance, displays many decimals, or compresses operational values into abbreviated numbers.
- **Author’s account:** A measure can be accurate yet communicate the wrong message. When the task is to judge deviation from a target, explicitly showing the variance can be more useful than making viewers calculate it from separate values. Excess detail and unnecessary decimal places slow overview reading.
- **Rationale / application:** Identify whether the user needs magnitude, relative change, target deviation, rate, distribution or an exact value. Choose a measure and label that express that question directly, retaining underlying values when needed for scale or accountability. Define numerator, denominator, sign convention and period for derived measures. Round only to a precision that preserves the decision; provide exact detail where consequential. Distinguish percentage change from percentage-point change, and handle zero or missing denominators honestly.
- **Exception / conflict:** A percentage can exaggerate a change from a tiny base or conceal a large absolute impact. Reduced precision is inappropriate where a threshold, reconciliation, safety judgment or financial commitment depends on the exact value. Do not infer significance or certainty from decimal places. Denominator edge cases and percentage-point distinctions are the skill's computational checks, not explicit formulas supplied by this passage.
- **Review:** Can the intended judgment be made without avoidable arithmetic, and does the derived measure retain the context needed to avoid a misleading conclusion? Test boundary values, negative values, missing/zero denominators and rounding near thresholds when those states are possible.
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, sections3.3–3.4, PDF45–47. Figure3-8 inspected at PDF47: the display centers the target deviation at zero rather than requiring comparison of two moving lines. Source extraction can lose minus signs, so its numerical examples are not reused as calculation fixtures.

### DD-06 — Choose an encoding for the question, not for visual variety

- **Trigger:** Selecting charts for a dashboard, replacing tables with graphics, adding pies, bubbles, radar charts or maps, or trying to make repeated charts look less repetitive.
- **Author’s account:** A display medium must match the quantitative message. Position and length can make comparison clearer than angles or areas. A pie implies parts of a whole; independent probabilities do not form that whole. A geographic map is useful when spatial relationships matter, not merely because rows have regional names. Reusing an appropriate chart type saves the viewer from repeatedly changing interpretation strategies.
- **Rationale / application:** State the question before choosing the mark: exact lookup, ordered comparison, temporal change, composition or spatial relationship. Prefer a clear table for exact values and an appropriate chart when its pattern is the point. Check whether categories form a valid whole before using a part-to-whole encoding. Use geography only when location affects interpretation. Keep repeated questions visually consistent; vary the medium when the question or data structure genuinely changes.
- **Exception / conflict:** Few's blanket rejection of pie charts is the author's position; the skill should evaluate a requested chart by its task, valid data and comparison precision rather than impose an unsupported universal ban. Area or spatial encodings may serve other tasks, but do not treat them as equally precise for comparisons of close values. Preserve useful brand expression and approved choices while explaining an evidenced encoding problem. The source examples are expert critiques, not controlled comparative studies.
- **Review:** Does the chart's implied relationship actually exist in the data? Can the intended comparison be made without decoding unrelated shapes, memorizing a legend, or mistaking area for diameter? Is repeated visual treatment stable enough to reuse a reading strategy?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, sections3.5–3.6, PDF47–52. Figures3-9 and3-12/3-13 checked at PDF47 and49. Figure3-13's caption says actual/budgeted revenue, while its title and adjacent argument compare revenue and operating cost; retain the encoding argument, not the inconsistent caption as a data definition.

### DD-07 — Keep quantitative geometry truthful and important values visible

- **Trigger:** Bar chart baseline or truncated scales, decorative perspective, occluded marks, inconsistent axes, or a chart whose labels and marks imply different values.
- **Author’s account:** Bar length encodes magnitude, so a nonzero origin can distort proportional judgments. Decorative 3-D perspective makes values harder to read; even when depth represents a variable, occlusion can hide values. A well-chosen chart type still fails when scales, labels or its component design distort interpretation.
- **Rationale / application:** For magnitude bars, use a zero baseline and a scale consistent with the encoded values. If the question requires magnifying a small change, choose a mark whose position can support a clearly labeled restricted domain instead of silently truncating magnitude bars. Avoid unnecessary perspective and verify that every relevant mark remains visible. Align comparison axes and units when their geometry invites comparison; explicitly distinguish incompatible scales. Check the actual plot and numbers, not just the chart configuration.
- **Exception / conflict:** Zero-baseline guidance for magnitude bars does not imply every line or point chart must start at zero, or that interval/range marks encode magnitude from zero. Data with negative values needs an honest zero reference and signed extent. Purposeful 3-D spatial work is outside the source's typical business-chart case. Current accessibility and interaction requirements govern how equivalent information is exposed.
- **Review:** Does the visible mark encode the value it claims, with the intended baseline, units and domain? Can a viewer inspect every consequential value without hidden marks or perspective errors, and are visual comparisons valid across neighboring plots?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, sections3.7–3.8, PDF52–56. Figures3-23/3-24 checked at PDF55 and3-25 at PDF56. The latter visibly begins at $500,000, producing misleading length ratios; the skill's alternative-mark and range-bar qualifications extend the passage to avoid overgeneralizing its rule.

### DD-08 — Reserve emphasis for meaningful differences and consequential conditions

- **Trigger:** Dashboards with competing card headers, multiple bright palettes, prominent chrome, arbitrary chart colors, or related measures placed far apart.
- **Author’s account:** Arrangement should follow importance and use: compare related items together and make consequential information stand out. If every element is equally prominent, the display cannot guide attention. Color differences invite interpretation and repeated colors imply relationships. Ornamental realism, heavy borders and decorative imagery can compete with data, while unattractive presentation also undermines use.
- **Rationale / application:** Establish a calm baseline and a small set of consistent emphasis treatments for real conditions. Group content by its meaning and intended comparisons; arrange paired actual/target values together and align related histories where useful. Subdue headings, controls and branding enough that they support orientation without competing with alerts. Use labels or other cues alongside status color, and keep the same semantic meaning across relevant charts. Make data presentation polished through legible type, spacing and coherent relationships rather than arbitrary chart variety.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not turn the author's strong aesthetic opinions into a ban on personality, logos, gradients or all decoration throughout the product. A simulation may legitimately resemble equipment. A fixed top-left priority rule depends on reading direction, layout and use; the task's actual attention needs govern placement. Color-vision prevalence figures and claims that decoration inevitably becomes annoying are dated source assertions, not fresh evidence. Exact contrast, assistive output and motion behavior require applicable current standards and runtime checks.
- **Review:** In the relevant task state, does the most consequential condition stand out for a reason, and can it be understood without relying only on color? Do grouping and repeated visual codes support the intended comparison rather than imply false relationships?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.3, sections3.7 and3.9–3.13, PDF53–63. Figures3-23 and3-26 inspected at PDF55 and57 to verify pairing and positional separation. This integrates the source's salience argument with the skill's existing visual craft and accessibility guidance, rather than replacing that guidance with a minimalism mandate.

### DD-09 — Choose visual attributes for the kind of comparison

- **Trigger:** Selecting marks, colors, sizes or positions to encode a measure or distinguish categories.
- **Author’s account:** Some visual differences attract attention before deliberate reading. Attributes differ in what they communicate: hue and shape distinguish kinds; position and length support quantitative comparison; intensity, area and width convey amount with limited precision. The circle-size example shows how difficult exact area ratios are to judge. Backgrounds can also change perceived color and intensity.
- **Rationale / application:** Start with the user's comparison: identify category, rank, estimate magnitude, or retrieve an exact value. Prefer a common position or length scale when small quantitative differences matter; use hue or shape for identity and redundant status cues. Label values when precision is required. Test marks on their actual background and at their delivered size. An ordered color scale may support overview, while an unordered palette cannot establish an honest numeric ranking by itself.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book's suggested small counts of discriminable attributes are heuristics, not universal palette, menu or chart limits. Area can be useful for overview and hierarchy despite its weaker precision. Do not infer that all intensity encodings work for every viewer, that hue has an inherent numeric order, or that a fast visual pop-out guarantees comprehension. Motion advice in the book does not justify flashing alerts; use current accessibility and platform requirements.
- **Review:** Can someone answer the intended comparison without estimating an unreliable area ratio or memorizing a legend? Are category identity, numerical amount and significance encoded distinctly? Does the encoding remain understandable without color alone?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.4, PDF67–74; Figures4-1/4-2 visually checked at PDF67 and Table4-2/Figure4-7 at PDF72. This is a contextual synthesis of the author's 2006 perception account, not a current universal performance benchmark.

### DD-10 — Group by relationships and reduce comparison memory

- **Trigger:** Arranging dashboard panels, tables, related measures or a process diagram.
- **Author’s account:** Limited short-term memory makes comparisons harder when information disappears. Proximity, similarity, enclosure, closure, continuity and connection can establish groups without repeated explanatory text. Connection and enclosure can dominate otherwise competing visual groupings.
- **Rationale / application:** Keep information needed for one comparison adjacent or simultaneously available where feasible. Use spacing and alignment to create a reading path; reserve stronger containment for meaningful boundaries. Repeat a visual treatment when items share a role. A connecting line implies a relationship, so name that relationship when it is not obvious. Make panel boundaries follow the user's task and the information's meaning rather than a uniform card grid imposed for appearance.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not convert the author's memory figures into an arbitrary item limit, or his simultaneous-view preference into a prohibition on scrolling, zoom, accessible reflow or detail views. Preserve the comparison through pinned context, a summary or a dedicated comparison view when one screen cannot reasonably contain it. Similar-looking metrics must not imply shared units or interchangeable meaning when they differ.
- **Review:** What must the person remember while moving between these items? Does whitespace imply the intended groups? Could a border, shared color or connecting line suggest a false relationship?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.4, PDF65–67,74–79. Figures4-18/4-19 visually checked at PDF79; the examples demonstrate competing grouping cues, not a measured universal ranking for every layout.

### DD-11 — Separate enduring priority from temporary exceptions

- **Trigger:** Establishing dashboard hierarchy, alert states or updates to a frequently revisited monitoring view.
- **Author’s account:** Some information is consistently important; other information becomes important only under current conditions. Stable placement supports familiar scanning, while changes in visual emphasis can draw attention to a temporary exception. Excessive highlighting destroys the contrast that gives highlighting its value.
- **Rationale / application:** Put recurring priorities in dependable locations and use a restrained, consistent exception vocabulary. Define what each signal means, the relevant threshold or comparison, and whether increasing the metric is favorable. Keep ordinary values legible while distinguishing actionable exceptions. As an implementation adaptation, pair color with text, shape or another accessible cue, and expose the state semantically. An up arrow identifies direction only when the period and comparison are clear; it does not independently mean good.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not rearrange the whole dashboard on every refresh just to promote the latest alert. Intentionally ranked exception queues can reorder when their ordering is visible and interaction remains stable. The book's preferred reading positions depend on reading direction and task. Its intensity-only accessibility claims and suggestion of at most two alert levels are not guarantees or quotas. Required controls, active filters and critical values must remain discoverable and sufficiently contrasted.
- **Review:** Can a returning user find the same measure? Can they explain the highlighted condition and its severity without color? Is the most vivid element actually the one requiring attention?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.4 PDF73–74; ch.5 PDF96–100; ch.6 PDF131–133. PDF96 visually checked for the distinction between persistent and situational priority. Accessibility checks are application qualifications rather than claims of standards compliance from this book.

### DD-12 — Remove visual interference while preserving useful scaffolding

- **Trigger:** Simplifying an overloaded table, graph or dashboard, or applying a minimalist visual direction.
- **Author’s account:** Drawing on Tufte's data-ink concept, Few recommends reducing noninformative marks and unnecessary data while emphasizing what remains. The qualification is functional: axes, grids, shading and boundaries can help interpretation and should be reduced or softened rather than automatically eliminated when useful.
- **Rationale / application:** Remove ornament that competes with interpretation, such as simulated depth with no data meaning, redundant borders and decorative gradients. Then check what alignment, labels, gridlines and grouping cues the task needs. A faint grid can support cross-panel comparisons; restrained row shading can support tracking in a wide table. Evaluate changes by the user's ability to read and compare accurately, not by a numerical ink ratio or an aesthetic claim that less is always better.
- **Exception / conflict:** Data versus nondata is not the same as useful versus useless. Do not erase zero lines, scope, units, uncertainty, thresholds, source freshness or control affordances to make the screen look quieter. The book's advice to mute controls or put them toward the bottom right is contextual, not a rule for filters and actions essential to the task. Light styling must still satisfy current accessibility requirements at actual size.
- **Review:** Which specific task does each remaining line, label, background and control support? After simplification, can users still locate the scope, follow a row, estimate values and distinguish groups? Did visual quiet make an essential state invisible?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.5, PDF80–94. Figures5-18/5-19 visually checked at PDF91: the redesign retains helpful panel structure and grids while reducing their weight. The author explicitly qualifies the data-ink aim; it is not a universal ban on grids, borders or images.

### DD-13 — Summarize for the decision without hiding the underlying situation

- **Trigger:** Choosing dashboard detail, aggregation, historical windows or an exception-only presentation.
- **Author’s account:** Dashboards need summaries matched to their audience's objectives and precision needs. They can emphasize recent detail and older summaries. Showing only exceptions is insufficient when users also need an overall picture of normal conditions; a lone traffic light cannot substitute for useful context.
- **Rationale / application:** Specify the decision, measure definition, population, unit, period and useful comparison before reducing detail. Select aggregation deliberately: total, average, range and count answer different questions. Retain enough baseline and history to interpret both normal and abnormal states. If recent daily values accompany earlier monthly or annual summaries, separate or visibly mark those granularities. Distinguish an incomplete current period from a completed comparison period; use appropriate comparable periods or explain the mismatch.
- **Exception / conflict:** Executive overview and transaction reconciliation demand different precision. Rounding should match the decision and retain exact values when required. An apparently stable aggregate can hide subgroups, tails or missing observations; add distribution or segmentation when those change the action. Additional periods are not useful merely because data exists. The book's preference for summary does not authorize discarding detail needed for diagnosis or audit.
- **Review:** What could this summary conceal that would reverse the decision? Is the state still interpretable when no alerts exist? Are windows, aggregation and partial-period comparisons apparent without opening several unrelated screens?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.5, PDF81–83,94–96. Figure5-24 visually checked at PDF96: yearly, monthly and daily histories occupy separately titled panels; the different intervals are not silently joined into one continuous time scale. Partial-period and missing-data checks are implementation applications.

### DD-14 — Select text or graphics by the question, not the dashboard label

- **Trigger:** Choosing among a number, table, chart, map or treemap for a dashboard item.
- **Author’s account:** Text is strong for looking up specific values; graphs expose relationships and patterns that a table may obscure. A compact library of display types should serve communication, not vendor novelty. Maps help when physical relationships matter. Treemaps combine hierarchy, area and color to reveal patterns across many items but do not support precise numerical comparison well.
- **Rationale / application:** Use text or an aligned table for exact lookup and verification; use a chart for a specified pattern or comparison; combine them when both matter. A number plus an explicit target or delta can be sufficient. Choose a map when adjacency, distance or location changes the decision, rather than merely because records contain place names. Choose a treemap for a meaningful part-to-whole hierarchy and overview; provide units, size/color definitions and a precise route to details.
- **Exception / conflict:** Tables may legitimately contain sparklines or bullet graphs. A graph is not mandatory on every card. Treemap prominence identifies a candidate for investigation, not automatically the greatest business loss: revenue area plus percent-of-quota color alone does not establish every measure of impact. Use bars or a table for accurate ranking. The author's criticisms of pies, radar and area graphs explain comparison weaknesses; they should not become unqualified bans divorced from task or natural data structure.
- **Review:** State the exact question this representation answers. Can users retrieve an exact value if necessary? Does geographic placement or nested area add information the decision actually uses? Could a simpler representation make the intended comparison more reliable?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF102–107,128–138, especially PDF134–137 for images, diagrams, tables and maps. Figure6-43 visually checked at PDF129; revenue uses rectangle area and percentage of quota uses red intensity. Historical vendor capability statements are not current product facts.

### DD-15 — Make compact performance comparisons honest

- **Trigger:** Bullet graph or gauge selection; displaying actual performance against a target, acceptable range or forecast.
- **Author’s account:** A bullet graph combines a labeled measure, quantitative scale, comparison marker and qualitative background ranges in little space. A bar requires a zero baseline because its length represents amount. For a narrowed range, Few substitutes a point for the bar. He also separates current actual performance from projected end-period performance.
- **Rationale / application:** Use a compact linear comparison when it answers the user's question clearly. State the metric, unit, period, target meaning and any threshold definitions. If length encodes amount, start at zero; if an expanded nonzero range is necessary, use a position mark with an explicit scale. Show actual and forecast as distinguishable quantities and state the forecast basis where relevant. Compare period-to-date performance with a period-appropriate expectation rather than treating distance from a future final target as proof of failure.
- **Exception / conflict:** Threshold bands need a defensible source; do not invent bad/acceptable/good ranges to fill a component. Lower values may be better, and target crossing can be harmful. The book's preferred number of bands is not a universal limit. Bullet graphs are a useful option, not a required replacement for every gauge or number. Few explicitly describes his comparison experiments as too small to support scientific performance claims.
- **Review:** Is the bar baseline honest? Can the user distinguish actual, target, acceptable range and projection? Are target period, direction of improvement and units clear? Would removing unsupported bands make the display more truthful?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF107–111. Figures6-6/6-7 visually checked at PDF108; Figures6-13/6-14 at PDF111. The nonzero-axis example uses a point, and the projection example distinguishes actual from expected future amount. Treat the author's 2006 informal experiment as limited evidence.

### DD-16 — Let the relationship determine bars, lines and composition

- **Trigger:** Comparing categories, changes over time, parts of a total or two related measures.
- **Author’s account:** Bars support individual values across discrete categories; lines emphasize connected change. Stacked bars expose totals and composition but make segments without a common baseline harder to compare. Combining marks should serve a meaningful relationship. A Pareto chart is a justified exception to avoiding lines across categories because its line represents ordered cumulative contribution.
- **Rationale / application:** Use bars for categorical magnitude comparisons and lines when successive values have meaningful continuity. For precise component comparison, prefer aligned bars or small multiples over floating stacked segments. Make composition denominators explicit. Keep units and scales clear; align panels with a shared scale when direct magnitude comparison matters. A line can use a nonzero scale to show changes when the range is apparent; zero-based bars still require zero. A Pareto curve must show actual cumulative values in a declared order, not assume an 80/20 outcome.
- **Exception / conflict:** Chart types are means, not absolute rules: a cumulative relationship justifies connected categories, and coarse composition may justify stacking. The book recommends dual-axis combinations in some examples, but independently chosen scales can imply arbitrary relationships. Prefer a shared unit/scale or aligned separate panels when possible; if dual axes are essential, label the mapping and avoid interpreting crossings or slopes as evidence of a relationship. Avoid silently joining gaps or irregular observations as if equally spaced measurements existed.
- **Review:** What does each connection mean? Which segments have a common baseline? Could the same data look strongly related merely by changing one axis? Are scale, ordering, intervals and denominator visible?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF111–120. Figures6-24/6-25 visually checked at PDF117 and Figure6-28 at PDF119. Dual-axis and gap cautions are application qualifications of the source's chart recommendations.

### DD-17 — Give miniature history enough context to mean something

- **Trigger:** Adding a sparkline, trend arrow or compact historical display beside a current value.
- **Author’s account:** Sparklines provide a quick historical pattern rather than exact quantitative reading. A reference band and a marked endpoint can enrich that context. A direction arrow can be ambiguous when the period of change is unspecified.
- **Rationale / application:** Pair a miniature trend with its metric, time window and current value; explain the relevant baseline or acceptable band when used. Use an endpoint cue only when it corresponds to the labeled current observation. Make the scale policy clear when readers compare multiple sparklines. Preserve missing-data gaps, distinguish a partial current period and provide a fuller view or values when precision is needed. These implementation details keep a small graphic from becoming a decorative assertion of momentum.
- **Exception / conflict:** An axis-free sparkline can be appropriate for a within-series shape question, but it does not establish exact magnitude or cross-series amplitude by itself. The author's claim that a sparkline removes ambiguity needs this qualification: an unlabeled window or independently rescaled series can still mislead. An arrow can work when its comparison and meaning are explicit; it is not intrinsically bad.
- **Review:** Over what period is this trend? Does the current label match the final mark? Are two apparently similar slopes actually comparable? Can someone distinguish zero, missing observations and a not-yet-complete period?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF120–121. Figures6-31/6-32/6-33 visually checked at PDF121, including the reference-band sparkline, ambiguous arrow and line-plus-value example. Missing-data and responsive reading checks are applications.

### DD-18 — Show variation when a center value conceals the decision

- **Trigger:** Monitoring compensation, latency, defects, delivery times or any measure where tails, spread or subgroups matter.
- **Author’s account:** A single average or median can conceal materially different distributions. Range marks and box plots add information about spread and position; the source illustrates quartiles, a median, whiskers and observations outside the whiskers, while acknowledging box-plot variants.
- **Rationale / application:** Decide whether the task concerns typical experience, dispersion, tail risk or individual exceptions. Add the distribution view needed for that question and define its population, sample count and period. Explain box, whisker and outlier conventions rather than assuming every chart library uses the same definition. Preserve consequential extreme observations and inspect subgroups before concluding that an acceptable average represents everyone.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book's statement that a median is the center of the range is imprecise: it is the ordered middle (the 50th percentile), not necessarily the midpoint of minimum and maximum. Its illustrated percentile whiskers are one convention, not the universal box-plot definition. Simplifying a plot for overview must not erase the tails the task is meant to detect; a small sample may be clearer as individual observations.

### DD-19 — Show associations without implying causation or confusing slope with strength

- **Trigger:** Investigating relationships between paired numerical measures or comparing relationships across groups.
- **Author’s account:** Scatter plots can reveal relationships more directly than separate time series. Few adds groups and fitted lines to make their patterns visible and favors simple displays for quick dashboard use.
- **Rationale / application:** Plot genuinely paired observations with explicit units and population; inspect direction, shape, spread, clusters and unusual points. Keep grouping meaningful and label the series. Add a fitted line only if its model is suitable and aids the question; distinguish observed points from a model. Use a deeper view when subgroup structure or uncertainty cannot be represented honestly in a compact summary.
- **Exception / conflict:** Association is not evidence of causation. A steeper fitted line measures a different change per unit; it does not by itself indicate stronger correlation. Do not repeat the source's description of the steeper example as a more positive correlation. The book's preference for straight fits is not a reason to hide a nonlinear pattern. Axis changes can alter apparent slope, and aggregation or shared time trends can complicate interpretation.
- **Review:** Are the observations paired correctly? Does the fit describe the points rather than impose a story? Would labels, subgroups, changed units or axis limits alter the claimed interpretation? Is a causal conclusion unsupported by this display?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF126–128. Figure6-41 visually checked at PDF127: TV and radio groups have different fitted slopes, which alone do not establish different correlation strength. The statistical qualifications are deliberate corrections, not additional claims attributed to Few.

### DD-20 — Use small multiples as a controlled comparison

- **Trigger:** Comparing the same measures across regions, channels, cohorts or periods without overloading one plot.
- **Author’s account:** Repeating a basic graph while varying one dimension enables many comparisons in a compact area. Shared labels and legends can reduce redundant reading when the arrangement keeps their meaning clear.
- **Rationale / application:** Hold the visual grammar steady: measure definitions, units, mark meanings, category ordering and scale policy. Vary the dimension the user needs to compare. Align panels and use common scales when magnitude comparison is intended. Keep enough labels for every panel to remain interpretable in its actual layout, including wrapped or narrow-screen arrangements. If independent scales reveal within-panel patterns, declare that choice and avoid claims of direct magnitude comparison.
- **Exception / conflict:** A repeated chart is not comparable merely because it looks consistent. Different time windows, denominators or populations can invalidate the intended comparison. Remove duplicated labels only when readers can still associate them reliably; responsive stacking may require repeating them. The author's expectation of future vendor support is historical, not an implementation constraint, and his example does not prove that one chart can never represent another dimension.
- **Review:** What stays constant across panels, and what changes? Can users compare the intended values without mentally translating scales or relocating missing labels? Does the layout preserve these conditions after reflow?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.6, PDF136–138. Figures6-56/6-57 visually checked at PDF137: separate channel panels retain repeated numeric scales and a shared series legend. PDF138 contains chapter6's concluding warning against adopting a visualization solely because a vendor offers it.

### DD-21 — Arrange the overview around the relationships people need to use

- **Trigger:** A monitoring screen contains many metrics, operational areas, business entities or related charts.
- **Author’s account:** Useful grouping can follow functions, entities or how the viewer uses the information. A person overseeing several departments may need a more integrated arrangement than the organizational structure. Related items should be close, with whitespace or restrained separators defining the groups.
- **Rationale / application:** Identify the intended scan and comparison tasks, then group and order measures to support them. Place the information needed to interpret a signal near that signal. Use consistent alignment and spacing before adding panels, borders or fills; retain subtle boundaries when dense content needs them. On responsive layouts preserve important comparison groups rather than merely stacking arbitrary component cards.

- **Review:** Can the intended user follow the relevant overview-to-exception sequence without searching distant panels for the comparator or context? Does the grouping survive the actual viewport and text-size conditions?

### DD-22 — Make the comparison meaningful before making it visually easy

- **Trigger:** Comparing measures across panels, normalizing to targets, combining different units, or placing trends side by side.
- **Author’s account:** Positioning related measures together, sharing a graph where appropriate, and supplying ratios or variances can make comparison easier. The sales examples distinguish exact amounts from relative performance; a dashboard consisting only of percentages may omit the magnitude a manager needs. The author also illustrates a dual-axis chart for differently measured series.
- **Rationale / application:** Name the comparison and preserve its meaning: metric definition, population, unit, time window, aggregation and reference value. Use common scales for comparable magnitudes; if each panel is scaled or normalized differently, make that explicit and do not imply equal visual heights mean equal amounts. Show actual magnitude alongside relative attainment when both affect the decision. A percent-of-target display compares attainment, not equal business impact. Keep forecast, pipeline and actual values distinguishable.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not adopt the book's dual-axis example as a default: two arbitrary scale choices can create a false impression of agreement or relationship. Prefer aligned separate plots or a clearly explained normalization when they answer the task more honestly; justify a dual axis if retained. A time-normalized trend and a magnitude comparison are different tasks, and a universal shared numeric scale can hide useful variation. These scale and comparison-contract checks extend the source's contextual reasoning; static graphs cannot establish correlation or cause.
- **Review:** Could someone infer an incorrect relationship because scales, denominators, periods or definitions differ? Can they distinguish relative attainment from actual amount and an observed result from an expectation?

### DD-23 — Keep visual meaning consistent across the whole dashboard

- **Trigger:** Multiple teams or chart components contribute colors, status treatments, axes, legends or interaction styles to one dashboard.
- **Author’s account:** Repeated appearances encourage viewers to infer repeated meanings. One example reuses yellow for satisfactory performance, forecast values and the month of June, misleadingly linking unrelated concepts. Unexplained variation in chart choice or styling also demands interpretation without adding information.
- **Rationale / application:** Define a local encoding contract: what color, shape, intensity, line style and position mean, where those meanings apply, and how status differs from category. Use the same display family for comparable relationships and tasks. Reserve strong attention cues for actual priorities and give meaningful status a textual or other perceivable cue. Inspect the assembled dashboard, not only each individually reasonable widget.
- **Exception / conflict:** Consistency does not mean every measure must use the same graph or every page the same composition. Different questions may require different encodings. A scoped legend can explain legitimate local differences, but repeatedly changing the meaning of a prominent status color still imposes a cost. The author's beliefs that certain historical hues remain distinguishable to color-blind viewers are not accessibility verification.
- **Review:** If the same visual cue appears in two sections, does it communicate the same meaning or clearly establish a different scope? Can key states be identified without depending on hue alone?

### DD-24 — Apply visual restraint without sacrificing legibility or useful character

- **Trigger:** Dense metrics become visually exhausting, tables are too small, ornament competes with data, or a redesign strips away all personality in the name of simplicity.
- **Author’s account:** Dashboard aesthetics should support communication, with clear imagery, readable type and restrained color. Saturated or dark elements attract attention and can compete with important values. His examples criticize unnecessary shadows, gradients, backgrounds and decorative photos, while also warning that excessive whitespace can make the actual information harder to read.
- **Rationale / application:** Spend visual emphasis on the information and relationships needed for the decision. Reduce heavy containers, repeated decoration and unnecessary effects when they compete with comparison. Check labels, numbers, units, warnings and actual chart marks at the intended size; enlarge important data rather than preserving empty margins. Keep intentional brand character where it does not distort values, crowd context or obscure priorities. Evaluate contrast, text scaling and supported appearances in the realized interface.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book's preferred off-white canvas, muted nature colors and restricted font palette are contextual design preferences, not universal requirements or a ban on dark mode. A photo or illustration can be task information, not mere decoration. Do not lower contrast until boundaries or marks disappear, or shrink text to satisfy the one-screen ideal. Current platform/accessibility requirements and the project's accepted visual direction govern implementation.
- **Review:** Does the visual emphasis lead to decision-relevant information, and is that information comfortably readable under actual display conditions? Which removed or retained element has a concrete role?

### DD-25 — Connect the overview to details through a coherent interaction contract

- **Trigger:** Dashboard drill-down, filters or detail navigation after an exception; the overview needs to lead into investigation or action.
- **Author’s account:** The monitoring overview often needs complementary information for diagnosis and response. Drilling into a measure and narrowing the displayed data are common interactions. The author favors direct activation of the relevant data and consistent launch behavior, and illustrates hover for precise point values.
- **Rationale / application:** Specify what a row, bar, point or alert opens and what metric, entity, date range and filter context travel with it. Distinguish drilling down from changing the overview's scope. Make interactive marks discoverable and provide a usable keyboard, touch and assistive route, such as an adjacent link or accessible detail control. Define return behavior so investigation does not require reconstructing the original selection or losing the signal being checked. Ensure displayed actions correspond to real available detail or tools.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not make tiny graphical marks or hover the sole way to obtain necessary values or actions. Explicit controls can be clearer and more accessible than the source's preference for eliminating separate buttons. An overview can legitimately be static when the job does not require interaction. State preservation, accessibility and capability checks are this skill's extensions; a static source image does not show them working.

### DD-26 — Validate interpretation with realistic tasks and state the coaching boundary

- **Trigger:** A dashboard introduces unfamiliar encodings, a team claims it is intuitive, or a polished prototype is being treated as evidence of user comprehension.
- **Author’s account:** The author recommends evaluating a prototype populated with real data by observing intended users. For unfamiliar display media he proposes first explaining how the display works. He also recommends presenting only one preferred design and predicts that good design usually needs modest refinement.
- **Rationale / application:** State the actual question before choosing the evaluation: unaided detection, correct interpretation, comparison, finding a cause, or performance after training. Use realistic data and neutral tasks. Observe what participants identify, misread and choose to investigate; separate preference from correct interpretation. If instruction is provided, record it and limit conclusions to assisted or learned use. If participant access is absent, perform honest expert/source/runtime checks and retain comprehension as a hypothesis.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not make one-prototype-only a rule, block requested alternatives, or treat a user's disagreement as ignorance. Matched alternatives can test a real uncertainty. Do not assume only minor changes will be needed or coach someone and then claim first-use discoverability. Neither an agent walkthrough nor agreement with the author's examples is a participant study; no recruitment or external contact is authorized by this source.
- **Review:** What did actual participants correctly detect and interpret, under what task and instruction conditions? If no such study occurred, is the report explicit about what remains untested?
- **Source / dating:** Ch.7 §7.5, PDF146; ch.8 introduction and sales comparison setup, PDF147–150. The source's single-design and minor-refinement claims are qualified above, not silently adopted. The dashboard contest examples are design arguments, not controlled experiments.

### DD-27 — Separate live, stale, paused, acknowledged and resolved states

- **Trigger:** Operational monitoring, frequent refreshes, temporary display freezing, alerts, or a dashboard mixing strategic and near-real-time information.
- **Author’s account:** The CIO example mixes a near-real-time alert strip with slower strategic information. The telesales example allows the viewer to freeze updates while examining a problem, makes the frozen state visible, and separately allows urgent alert signals to be silenced and reset. The author proposes blinking and optional audio for urgency.
- **Rationale / application:** Specify freshness and update cadence per relevant section rather than imply the whole dashboard is equally current. If pausing is useful, show that data is frozen and its observation time; define resume behavior and what happens to newly arriving events. Keep alert acknowledgment/silencing distinct from the underlying condition becoming normal. Make missing, stale or failed input distinguishable from a verified healthy state. Choose attention mechanisms by urgency and actual workflow, with accessible and user-controllable alternatives.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not automatically adopt blinking, escalating sound, or auto-reset behavior from the 2006 examples. Critical monitoring may require alerts to remain active while the visual snapshot is paused; confirm the domain's contract instead of inventing it. The source's statement that no red circles means nothing is wrong assumes functioning current input. Timestamps, stale-data states and distinct acknowledgment/resolution semantics are implementation safeguards derived from the example, not explicit book specifications.
- **Review:** Can someone tell whether the value is current, stale or intentionally frozen, and whether an alert was silenced or actually resolved? Have update failure, pause/resume and new-alert behavior been exercised rather than merely drawn?

### DD-28 — Audit decision coverage before judging the dashboard's polish

- **Trigger:** Reviewing a complete dashboard, comparing design alternatives, or accepting a concise display that may hide important context.
