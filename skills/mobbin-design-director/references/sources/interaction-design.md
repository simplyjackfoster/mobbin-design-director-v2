# Interaction Design: Beyond Human–Computer Interaction — contextual source lessons

Yvonne Rogers, Helen Sharp and Jennifer Preece. Sixth edition, Wiley, 2023.
ISBN 9781119901099. The supplied title/copyright pages identify this edition.
One-based PDF page = printed Arabic page +26 in the body.
[Chapter and visual coverage](interaction-design-coverage.json).

All 16 chapters and the epilogue were read in bounded full-text batches by three
readers. Thirty-eight selected visual pages were rendered and inspected, including
figures, tables, one conceptual-model box and one equation. This does not claim
that every image or underlying cited study was independently audited. Original
PDF, page text and rendered book pages remain outside Git.

The source adds emotional context, conceptual-model precision, prototype fidelity,
question-specific representation and evaluation limits. Existing AF/UX/JJ/DI/WI/TD
rules remain the primary routes where these overlap; these cards supply provenance
and qualifications rather than extra mandatory steps. Source claims are separated
from our operational adaptations. Screenshots establish visible features, not
participant feelings, actual transitions or causal improvements.

## Reconcile, do not universalize

- Emotional expression can help or interfere according to the moment, audience and
  repetition. The source's critique of unsolicited assistance does not ban passive
  resident characters. Pleasant appearance does not prove functional usability.
- Metaphors can help selected relationships and mislead elsewhere. This source
  explicitly acknowledges opposition to metaphor; a decorative theme need not
  become a literal interaction model.
- Recognition is useful when it saves effort; entering a known value can beat a
  long picker. Working-memory findings do not establish visible-menu quotas.
- Prototype fidelity has multiple dimensions. A polished screenshot can answer a
  visual question while leaving behavior unknown. High fidelity can be necessary
  for the particular uncertainty under investigation.
- Popularity, pattern reuse, evaluator agreement and aggregate scores do not prove
  task fit. The source's fixed counts, historical detection rates and simplified
  statistical explanations are not adopted as operational thresholds.

### ID-01 — Define experience quality beyond task completion

- **Trigger:** Framing a new experience or evaluating an apparently successful conversion.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Usability and experience goals differ; tasks, context, culture, access and unwanted experiences affect what good means. Digitization can add device, attention, access and privacy burdens.
- **Our application:** State the intended outcome and desired feeling at consequential moments. Include meaningful access and contextual constraints; distinguish product success from user benefit.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not turn broad audience assertions into stereotypes or impose pleasure on serious moments. A minimal interface can retain useful character and expressive detail.
- **Review:** Can a successful completion still leave the person confused, excluded, pressured or unsure what happened?
- **Source / dating:** Ch1 pp8–10,14–31 (PDF34–36,40–57). Historical examples are illustrative, not current prevalence claims.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-01, UX-16, AF-01.

### ID-02 — Design the exposed conceptual model before the screens

- **Trigger:** New navigation, unfamiliar objects, or a feature organized like backend services.
- **Source principle / rationale:** A conceptual model identifies concepts, attributes, operations, relationships and mappings. The system image mediates the designer’s intention and the user’s understanding.
- **Our application:** Name objects, meaningful states, allowed actions and relationships in user language. Check that each destination has a distinguishable job and each action exposes its consequence. This compact model is our operational adaptation.
- **Exception / conflict:** A model is a hypothesis, not proof of comprehension. Technical details belong in the interface only when they help a decision.
- **Review:** Could a person predict what an object contains, which action changes it, and where the result will appear?
- **Source / dating:** Ch3 pp75–78,94 (PDF101–104,120); ch11 pp410–414 (PDF436–440). Box3.5/PDF120 visually checked.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-06, AF-01, JJ-14.

### ID-03 — Use metaphors as limited bridges and manage their promises

- **Trigger:** Wallets, cards, assistants, mascots, or other familiar imagery suggesting unfamiliar behavior.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Metaphors can help people understand selected concepts but may constrain growth or import inappropriate expectations. Anthropomorphic and metaphorical representations can suggest competence beyond actual capability.
- **Our application:** Specify which relationship the metaphor clarifies and where it stops. A character may orient or accompany a task without pretending to understand, monitor or guarantee outcomes. Define that role before drawing its pose or dialogue.
- **Exception / conflict:** The book acknowledges Cooper’s objections to metaphor; preserve this tension rather than prescribing metaphor everywhere. Decorative imagery need not become a literal operating model.
- **Review:** What capability does this representation imply, and does the actual behavior support that expectation?
- **Source / dating:** Ch3 pp79–81 (PDF105–107); ch6 pp192–195 (PDF218–221); ch12 pp461–466 (PDF487–492). Fig3.6/PDF106 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-06, AF-13, UX-16.

### ID-04 — Choose an interaction form by its effort and repair path

- **Trigger:** Considering chat, forms, gestures, direct manipulation or automation for a task.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Interaction types describe what people do; interface types describe how this is implemented. Conversation, instruction, manipulation and exploration each have costs, and system inference can be wrong.
- **Our application:** Compare repeated-task effort, discoverability, correction and closure. Give inferred or conversational actions a clear way to inspect, correct, ignore or retry their result.
- **Exception / conflict:** Natural-looking interaction is not inherently easy. A deterministic form can be more efficient than dialogue; unfamiliar gestures may require learning.
- **Review:** Can the person recover from a mistaken inference without restarting or learning hidden syntax?
- **Source / dating:** Ch3 pp81–88 (PDF107–114); ch5 pp141–146 (PDF167–172); ch7 pp232–237,262–268 (PDF258–263,288–294). Fig3.7/PDF109 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-12, AF-13, JJ-14.

### ID-05 — Externalize state without inventing item-count limits

- **Trigger:** Dense summaries, interrupted tasks, multi-step forms or a proposed maximum number of visible options.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Visible information can be rescanned; working-memory results do not impose a seven-item limit on menus. External representations support remembering, calculation and annotation.
- **Our application:** Keep selected scope, progress, recent result, entered data and return destination available where needed. Structure comparison rather than deleting useful data to satisfy an arbitrary count.
- **Exception / conflict:** Visibility has density and attention costs. A visible list is not automatically easy to scan, and repeating every detail everywhere can obscure priorities.
- **Review:** After interruption, can the person see where they are, what is retained and what remains to do?
- **Source / dating:** Ch4 pp103–111,115–116,127–132 (PDF129–137,141–142,153–158). Figs4.1/PDF130 and4.6/PDF152 inspected; the hotel examples are not an identical-content experiment.
- **Consolidate with:** JJ-07, JJ-08, UX-11.

### ID-06 — Match emotional expression to the moment and repeated use

- **Trigger:** Visual direction, empty states, completion, errors or recurring mascot appearances.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Emotion varies across an interaction. Appearance, skilled use and longer-term meaning are distinct lenses. Expressive agents can reassure, while unsolicited repeated help can annoy; aesthetic appeal can influence perceived usability without establishing correctness.
- **Our application:** Define the emotional job at each state: welcome, orient, reassure, explain or acknowledge. Separate passive character presence from interruptive advice. Compare the same task and content across directions and inspect recurring exposure as well as first impression.
- **Exception / conflict:** The Clippy account does not justify banning all resident mascots. Humor that fits browsing may trivialize uncertainty or financial loss. Owner taste and source examples do not prove the audience feels reassured.
- **Review:** Does expression support understanding and the right emotional tone without competing with status, action or recovery?
- **Source / dating:** Ch6 pp172–183,189–196 (PDF198–209,215–222). FiguresPDF204,207–209,220 inspected. Three-level diagram is a conceptual lens, not a validated implementation model of the brain.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-16, AF-06, UX-13.

### ID-07 — Represent awareness without assuming attention or consent

- **Trigger:** Shared status, social features, assistant messages or public participation indicators.
- **Source principle / rationale:** People coordinate through timing, turn-taking and awareness; visible presence does not guarantee attention. Public contribution measures can intimidate, and technologies alter social expectations.
- **Our application:** Define what a status actually means, who can see it and what response is expected. Offer discreet, accessible participation and sensible closure or deferred response.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not introduce social instrumentation merely because it is possible. Workplace or immersive examples do not establish needs in a personal finance app.
- **Review:** Could someone mistake a presence or activity cue for availability, agreement or a demand to respond?
- **Source / dating:** Ch5 pp136–146,147–168 (PDF162–172,173–194). Fig5.5/PDF175 inspected; behavior and attention are not established by the virtual-office image.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-09, UX-04.

### ID-08 — Select input controls around the answer people already know

- **Trigger:** Long pickers, search, forms or replacing typing with recognition controls.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Recognition often helps, but typing a known value can be easier than scanning a large list. Form design must support appropriate entry, correction and avoid unnecessary repetition.
- **Our application:** Use search, browse and sensible formats according to task uncertainty. Preserve entered values and make autofill or inferred values correctable. For mobile, verify the keyboard and selection behavior in the actual platform.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not infer nationality from location or treat recognition as universally superior. Historical country dropdowns and touch dimensions are not current native specifications.
- **Review:** Can a person who knows the answer enter it quickly, while someone uncertain can discover valid choices?
- **Source / dating:** Ch7 pp206–209,223–230 (PDF232–235,249–256). Figs7.5/PDF233 and7.6/PDF235 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-14, JJ-12, TD-07.

### ID-09 — Keep meaning available across presentation and access conditions

- **Trigger:** Icon-only actions, unfamiliar gestures, touch feedback or accessibility exploration.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Icons depend on familiarity and interpretation; mobile interaction cannot depend on hover. Disability can be permanent, temporary or situational. Simulations can miss coping skills and produce misleading assumptions about lived experience.
- **Our application:** Expose essential action meaning and a usable alternate route where required. Check semantics and actual assistive interaction; treat simulations as checks of specific constraints, not evidence about disabled people’s experiences.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not generalize the book’s color, target-size or demographic simplifications. Haptics, animation and pictures should not become the sole carrier of an essential message.
- **Review:** Can the supported task be understood and completed through the relevant access route, and which needs remain untested?
- **Source / dating:** Ch1 pp16–19 (PDF42–45); ch7 pp212–216,240–244 (PDF238–242,266–270); ch12 p457 (PDF483). Figs7.9–7.10/PDF240 inspected. Current platform details require current official guidance.
- **Consolidate with:** TD-07, TD-21, JJ-04.

### ID-10 — Fit evidence collection to the question and keep methods distinguishable

- **Trigger:** Research planning, source synthesis or a claim supported by several different observations.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Data gathering depends on goals, participants, recording and analysis. Interviews reveal reports; observation reveals behavior in its setting. Multiple methods can expose different perspectives without agreeing or measuring the same thing.
- **Our application:** Record the consequential question, evidence available, method and missing conditions. Keep observations, interpretations and recommendations separate; do not count unrelated screenshots, book claims and owner preferences as replicated user evidence.
- **Exception / conflict:** There is no universally sufficient interview or app count. Triangulation can reveal disagreement rather than remove it. Source sampling and legal/consent simplifications are not adopted.
- **Review:** Does each piece of evidence answer the claim it is being used to support?
- **Source / dating:** Ch8 pp274–293,302–312 (PDF300–319,328–338); ch9 pp326–333 (PDF352–359).
- **Consolidate with:** AF-02, UX-02, WI-08.

### ID-11 — Make research prompts and response controls valid for the claim

- **Trigger:** Preparing a survey, interview, preference question or structured visual comparison.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Question wording, ordering and response format shape what people can report. Leading, ambiguous or compound questions, overlapping ranges and mismatched controls distort results.
- **Our application:** Use neutral, concrete questions with one interpretable decision. Provide relevant alternatives and unknown or other responses when needed. Pilot both wording and branching; owner calibration should ask a focused preference question without pretending to be user research.
- **Exception / conflict:** Closed choices are useful when well scoped but cannot establish omitted explanations. Do not generalize book response-rate thresholds or treat all checkbox responses as independent people.
- **Review:** Could a reasonable answer be missing, forced into two ranges, or changed by the way the question is phrased?
- **Source / dating:** Ch8 pp284–301 (PDF310–327); fig8.7/PDF325 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-23, WI-08.

### ID-12 — Preserve evidence units, counterexamples and uncertainty during analysis

- **Trigger:** Counts, ratings, analytics, coded observations or claims of visual improvement.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Qualitative and quantitative analysis answer different questions. Interpretation depends on coding, distributions and context; critical incidents may reveal positive or negative experiences.
- **Our application:** Keep the unit, denominator, context and competing explanations with findings. Seek disconfirming cases. Report owner preference as preference and script success as exercised behavior; preserve difficult observations instead of hiding them in a mean.
- **Exception / conflict:** The source’s casual recommendation to remove outliers and its oversimplified statistics are not adopted. Agreement between coders does not establish truth, and a rating difference does not quantify objective beauty.
- **Review:** Would the conclusion change if an unusual case, missing response or alternative explanation were retained?
- **Source / dating:** Ch9 pp326–349,362–367 (PDF352–375,388–393). Fig9.3/PDF365 inspected. Underlying study results are not independently audited.
- **Consolidate with:** WI-08, UX-24, TD-06.

### ID-13 — Design summaries and visualizations for a particular question

- **Trigger:** Dashboards, scores, comparisons or dense financial summaries.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Representations serve different audiences and questions; visual literacy affects interpretation. An overview and a detailed spatial or linked representation can each be useful in their own setting.
- **Our application:** State what decision each summary supports, expose scope and freshness, and offer the detail needed to interpret or act. Keep selection and linked-view state clear. Evaluate comprehension separately from decorative appeal.
- **Exception / conflict:** Less data is not universally better; neither is more charting. Attractive illustrative data does not establish accuracy or meaningful scoring. Avoid misleading scales and visual encodings.
- **Review:** Can the intended person tell what this value covers, why it matters now, and what to do with it?
- **Source / dating:** Ch10 pp389–397, especially393–396 (PDF415–423, especially419–422). Figs10.14/PDF421 and10.15/PDF423 inspected; fig7.17/PDF254 provides a historical illustrated-app example only.
- **Consolidate with:** JJ-03, UX-15, DI-05.

### ID-14 — Treat inferred emotion and opaque scores as uncertain representations

- **Trigger:** Emotion detection, personalized recommendations, tracking or an interface that appears to know how someone feels.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Emotional signals and large datasets need interpretation; combining data can reveal more than expected. Explanations must be understandable for their audience, and persuasive feedback can backfire.
- **Our application:** Expose relevant inputs, scope, uncertainty and correction where decisions depend on inference. Minimize unnecessary collection. Do not use a mascot’s confidence or a decorative trust badge to imply verified certainty.
- **Exception / conflict:** A sentiment label or sensor output is not a direct reading of felt experience. Legal, fairness and liability simplifications in this book are excluded; current applicable requirements need independent verification.
- **Review:** Can the person understand and challenge the consequential inference without being shamed or misled?
- **Source / dating:** Ch6 pp184–191,195–196 (PDF210–217,221–222); ch10 pp371–388,398–404 (PDF397–414,424–430); ch11 pp414–415 (PDF440–441).
- **Consolidate with:** AF-09, JJ-16, UX-16.

### ID-15 — Specify requirements as behavior, data quality and use context

- **Trigger:** A screen brief, implementation contract or flow that seems complete only in the happy path.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Requirements evolve and include functionality, data, environmental conditions, user characteristics and experience goals. Work documents describe prescribed practice, which may differ from actual behavior.
- **Our application:** Capture relevant freshness, accuracy, persistence and environment alongside actions and content. Use observed work where available; mark hypotheses. Define alternate paths and system responsibilities instead of copying a source example’s incidental behavior.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not invent research personas or quotes. Requirements need enough detail for the decision, not exhaustive paperwork; genuine findings may revise them.
- **Review:** Which data or contextual assumption would make the apparently correct screen fail its real task?
- **Source / dating:** Ch11 pp407–424,426–439 (PDF433–450,452–465). Fig11.8/PDF458 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-02, AF-04, UX-05, UX-17.

### ID-16 — Trace the journey outside the interface and through alternate outcomes

- **Trigger:** Multi-screen work, interrupted setup or an experience whose success depends on events before and after the app.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Scenarios and journey maps include context, actions, emotions and touchpoints beyond a screen. Storyboards and use cases help reveal sequence and alternatives.
- **Our application:** Connect entry, decisions, carried state, completion and return destination. Include relevant interruption, correction, cancellation and recovery. Distinguish observed journeys, proposed stories and unresolved questions in the same compact task packet.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not mistake a polished journey map for evidence or require a large workshop artifact for a small change. A map and a wireflow answer related but different questions.
- **Review:** What happens immediately before entry, after completion and when the intended sequence is interrupted?
- **Source / dating:** Ch11 pp426–439 (PDF452–465); ch12 pp470–477 (PDF496–503). FiguresPDF458 and503 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-04, AF-04, AF-05.

### ID-17 — Choose prototype fidelity along the dimensions the question requires

- **Trigger:** Deciding whether to sketch, render, build natively or simulate an external service.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Fidelity includes appearance, data, functionality, interactivity and spatial qualities; breadth, depth and robustness are also distinct. A polished prototype may still omit critical behavior.
- **Our application:** Name the uncertainty, then specify which dimensions must be realistic and which are simulated. Use native behavior when system chrome, keyboard, gesture or restoration is the question; use real content and comparable fixtures when judging visual hierarchy.
- **Exception / conflict:** A narrow prototype is legitimate if claims stay narrow. High visual fidelity does not establish task reliability, and prototype success does not make code production-ready.
- **Review:** Which claim can this artifact actually establish, and what remains fake or untested?
- **Source / dating:** Ch12 pp441–456 (PDF467–482), especially Tables12.1–12.2/PDF479; ch2 pp61–64 (PDF87–90). Tables visually inspected. Native and deterministic-fixture prescriptions are our adaptations.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-18, UX-19, AF-06.

### ID-18 — Use negative scenarios to test a concept’s social and emotional cost

- **Trigger:** Promising new concepts, mascots, automation or features justified only by an optimistic story.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Positive and negative scenarios expose competing values and possible unintended consequences. Concrete representations help examine a concept before construction.
- **Our application:** Write the plausible successful scenario and a consequential failure or discomfort scenario. Use that contrast to revise behavior, framing and recovery before visual polish.
- **Exception / conflict:** These are design probes, not fabricated participant testimony or predictions of frequency. Avoid treating every speculative concern as a proven defect.
- **Review:** Under what plausible circumstance would the same delightful feature become intrusive, embarrassing or confusing?
- **Source / dating:** Ch12 pp458–462 (PDF484–488), fig12.9/PDF485 inspected.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-05, UX-16, WI-10.

### ID-19 — Treat pattern popularity as a search clue rather than a verdict

- **Trigger:** Mobbin references, design-system extensions or a request to copy a familiar screen.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Patterns connect problems, context and rationale. Common practice can still be poor practice; a pattern may become an antipattern as context or technology changes.
- **Our application:** Use references to compare equivalent decisions, explain mechanisms and identify tradeoffs. Record observed pixels separately from inferred behavior. Improve a borrowed pattern when its task fit, state behavior or access is weak.
- **Exception / conflict:** One app’s distinctive appearance is not a universal prescription, and several apps repeating a pattern are not independent usability studies. Historical implementation examples require current platform checks.
- **Review:** What problem does this pattern solve here, and which costs or missing states does the reference leave unresolved?
- **Source / dating:** Ch13 pp505–510 (PDF531–536), especiallyp509/PDF535; ch2 pp55–59 (PDF81–85). Fig13.5/PDF533 inspected. Mobbin protocol is our operational adaptation.
- **Consolidate with:** AF-08, TD-04, WI-10.

### ID-20 — Carry research into delivery without turning it into ceremony

- **Trigger:** Handoffs, subagents, long-running implementation or a design library growing faster than its use.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Agile and UX work can become disconnected; findings and broader experience concerns can disappear during local delivery. Useful documentation depends on its readers and ongoing collaboration.
- **Our application:** Attach the relevant principle and behavior criterion to the task or component contract, then verify the resulting interaction. Keep a single shared brief and selectively retrieve guidance. Revise the few affected criteria when evidence changes.
- **Exception / conflict:** This is our implementation of the book’s collaboration concern, not a prescribed sprint length, mandatory team structure or extra approval gate. Routine approved edits remain small.
- **Review:** Can the implementer and reviewer find the consequential reason and criterion at the moment they need it?
- **Source / dating:** Ch13 pp491–504,516–518 (PDF517–530,542–544); ch2 pp38–48 (PDF64–74).
- **Consolidate with:** AF-07, UX-25.

### ID-21 — Preserve useful strengths while resolving conflicts

- **Trigger:** Critique, redesign or synthesis across books with different emphases.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Evaluation and iterative design consider context, competing goals and alternative approaches; a prescription is not a recipe for all situations.
- **Our application:** Record what currently works and the conflict each change resolves. Prefer a reasoned contextual choice to accumulating all source rules. Keep unresolved uncertainty visible and check the affected behavior after revision.
- **Exception / conflict:** A consistent visual system can deliberately differentiate consequential actions. Neither minimalism nor expressiveness independently guarantees quality.
- **Review:** Which existing strength could this improvement damage, and what evidence justifies the tradeoff?
- **Source / dating:** Ch1 pp20–31 (PDF46–57); ch2 pp55–64 (PDF81–90); ch13 pp505–510 (PDF531–536). This cross-chapter reconciliation is our synthesis.
- **Consolidate with:** WI-10, UX-01, AF-06.

### ID-22 — Keep cultural and access context explicit when extending a product

- **Trigger:** Localization, new audiences, alternative inputs or transferring a successful example to another context.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Language, economics, culture, environment and access shape interaction; localization extends beyond translation. Broad stakeholder participation can reveal otherwise hidden constraints.
- **Our application:** Identify which assumptions travel and which require checking: vocabulary, formats, devices, connectivity, permissions and meaningful tasks. Preserve an accessible core route when richer presentation depends on a capability.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not replace evidence with national or demographic stereotypes. This does not require supporting every device or undertaking a new field study for every edit.
- **Review:** Which contextual assumption is least supported for the intended audience, and how does the design cope if it is wrong?
- **Source / dating:** Ch1 pp14–19 (PDF40–45); ch12 pp467–469 (PDF493–495); epiloguepp615–618 (PDF641–644).
- **Consolidate with:** TD-01, TD-07, UX-08.

### ID-23 — Match evaluation claims to evidence and setting

- **Trigger:** Planning an improvement comparison or describing what a prototype established.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Evaluation methods and settings reveal different aspects of usability and experience. Analytics shows activity without establishing motives; inspection predicts issues without participants. Controlled and natural settings offer different constraints and insights.
- **Our application:** State the claim, required evidence, available method and scope. Separate runtime behavior, expert inspection, owner preference, participant comprehension and long-term use. Record device, starting state, prompts, sample and missing conditions.
- **Exception / conflict:** A narrow check can be useful without generalizing. A simulator run does not establish hand comfort in ordinary mobile use; artificiality alone does not invalidate a scoped result.
- **Review:** Does the evidence measure the claimed improvement, and what consequential question remains unanswered?
- **Source / dating:** Ch14 pp520–526,530–539,545–546 (PDF546–552,556–565,571–572); ch15 pp550–558,563–574 (PDF576–584,589–600). FiguresPDF563–564,583–584,596 inspected by integrating lead.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-20, WI-07, UX-18.

### ID-24 — Use formative findings to change and recheck the design

- **Trigger:** Early visual alternatives, evolving flows or pilot findings.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Formative evaluation informs a changing design; summative evaluation assesses it against criteria. Opportunistic feedback can refine an early direction within its actual limits.
- **Our application:** Evaluate the uncertain mechanism at sufficient fidelity, make the warranted revision, and rerun affected checks. Keep owner calibration separate from participant outcomes.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not require a new approval checkpoint for every reversible change or generalize local feedback to all users.
- **Review:** Which decision changed or gained support, and was the revised path exercised?
- **Source / dating:** Ch14 pp524,532–533 (PDF550,558–559); ch15 pp550–558 (PDF576–584).
- **Consolidate with:** UX-24, WI-11, UX-18.

### ID-25 — Keep comparisons matched and samples proportionate to the question

- **Trigger:** Baseline-versus-redesign tasks, numeric claims or choosing an evaluation sample.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Controlled studies trade off learning, individual differences and sample needs; task, method and analysis affect what is sufficient. Counterbalancing can address order effects. Natural use may reveal difficulties a short test misses. Production experiments must keep the changed variable and comparison conditions aligned; a changed offer can confound an intended visual comparison.
- **Our application:** Hold content, starting state and task constant for the relevant comparison; identify changed variables and untested dimensions. Preserve denominator, order, difficult cases and uncertainty. Deterministic fixtures are our engineering adaptation, not a participant experiment. If multiple factors intentionally change, attribute findings to the package. Owner selection between two directions is not a randomized participant A/B test.
- **Exception / conflict:** Do not adopt a universal five-person rule or the source’s misleading p-value explanation. Seek suitable statistical expertise for inferential claims. Script correctness, preference and lived experience remain different outcomes.
- **Review:** What exactly improved, for whom, under which conditions and with which uncertainty?
- **Source / dating:** Ch15 pp554–558,565–568,573–574 (PDF580–584,591–594,599–600). Table15.1/PDF583 and apparatusPDF584 inspected. Ch16 pp607–610 (PDF633–636), fig16.5/PDF635 inspected; a headline-content change is not merely a color/font change.
- **Consolidate with:** TD-06, UX-21, WI-08.

### ID-26 — Scope actual participant involvement and interpret traces with context

- **Trigger:** Authorized recruitment, recording, interviews, analytics or interpreting online behavior.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Participants need meaningful information about participation and data handling. Visible traces need interpretation within surrounding practices; recording and prompts can alter what occurs.
- **Our application:** For actual studies, specify voluntary participation, data minimization and handling, and check consequential interpretations with suitable people where possible. Keep observation, interpretation and recommendation distinct.
- **Exception / conflict:** This does not authorize recruitment or tracking, and it does not add a consent ceremony to a local fixture inspection. Current applicable requirements supersede dated legal descriptions.
- **Review:** Were people and data treated as agreed, and could the apparent behavior have another contextual explanation?
- **Source / dating:** Ch14 pp543–544 (PDF569–570); ch15 pp578–581 (PDF604–607); ch8 pp279–281,302–312 (PDF305–307,328–338).
- **Consolidate with:** AF-02, UX-02, WI-07.

### ID-27 — Define analytics before interpreting success or failure

- **Trigger:** Drop-off, long sessions, engagement differences or a proposed analytics-based improvement claim.
- **Source principle / rationale:** Logs reveal patterns of activity at scale, while participant inquiry can reveal detailed problems and reasons. Large volumes of data can overwhelm interpretation; dashboards can mix scopes and units.
- **Our application:** State the event definition, task goal, population, time/filter scope and denominator. Track completion and recovery where relevant. Investigate alternative explanations: short sessions may reflect efficient success, and long sessions may reflect confusion. These are operational interpretations, not source claims of causality.
- **Exception / conflict:** No automatic tracking installation is authorized. Historical product instructions, bounce-rate bands and market shares are not current requirements or quality thresholds. Anonymization alone does not settle all ethical concerns.
- **Review:** What human goal does this metric represent, what is its denominator, and what else could produce the observed pattern?
- **Source / dating:** Ch16 pp601–606 (PDF627–632), fig16.4/PDF630–631 inspected. Panels show different time scopes and a language-table total699 versus overview2988; they must not be combined as one denominator.
- **Consolidate with:** UX-20, WI-08, TD-06.

## Assessed material not promoted as general guidance

All chapter prose, boxes, activities, interviews and further-reading annotations
were assessed. Exercises were source material, not instructions to contact people,
collect data, purchase products or follow linked sites. No body prose was excluded
from reading; the following claims or details were excluded from reusable rules.

- Ch1: broad adult/cartoon and color-blindness simplifications (pp16–18) are not
  audience evidence or an accessibility recipe. Ch2: sprint timing, legal examples
  and historical product claims are contextual, not required process or current law.
- Ch3–5: immersive hardware histories, broad attention percentages, cognitive or
  cultural generalizations and specialized social systems are not default phone
  requirements. Relevant task/coordination mechanisms remain in the cards.
- Ch6: the inaccurate HTTP404/HTML-syntax explanation (p183) is excluded. Emotion
  recognition accuracy, gendered preference claims, simple biological explanations
  and persuasion success do not become verified facts about this audience.
- Ch7: historical interface screenshots, device figures, target dimensions and
  voice/haptic assumptions require current official platform and actual-device
  verification. Specialized AR/VR/robotics machinery is not routine skill process.
- Ch8: simplified random/systematic sampling and generalization (p275), blanket
  consent claims involving disabled adults (p277), implicit consent (p309), dated
  caption-accuracy and response-rate thresholds are not adopted. Current research
  obligations need applicable verification when actual studies are authorized.
- Ch9: automatic outlier removal (p334), oversimplified mode/median claims (p331),
  and treating ratings or code agreement as objective truth are excluded. Specialist
  analysis notation/tools are contextual options, not mandatory techniques.
- Ch10: emotion inference, generalized fairness prescriptions, GDPR/AI-regulation
  conflation and legal-liability assertions (pp399–402) are not current operational
  guidance. Biomedical/surveillance examples do not authorize collection or tracking.
- Ch11–13: persona examples do not validate our audience; source bad-reset examples
  are not patterns to copy. Disability simulation is not lived-experience research.
  Brand anecdotes, framework/license descriptions and fixed sprint practices are
  not proof of effectiveness or present technical/legal instructions.

## Corroboration without additional workflow rules

Chapter16 materially supports existing inspection and touch guidance; reuse those
rules rather than adding duplicate mandatory checks:

- **WI-03, concrete criteria:** pp586,590,594–595 (PDF612,616,620–621) support
  translating a broad principle into a task-specific question. Our extension is
  to pair that question with a state/artifact and suitable verification method;
  a complete ledger checks bookkeeping, not whether a judgment is true.
- **WI-06, cognitive walkthrough:** pp595–598 (PDF621–624), Figure16.3/PDF623 and
  Table16.1/PDF624 distinguish forming an intention, noticing an action, mapping
  it to the goal and understanding feedback. Four conspicuous check-in choices
  can still leave a novice unsure which one applies. A walkthrough principally
  predicts learnability within its specified audience and task.
- **WI-04/07/08, independent and provisional inspection:** pp590–591,600–601
  (PDF616–617,626–627) describe missed problems, false alarms, independent passes
  and complementary participant methods. Our severity/confidence separation and
  verified-defect/risk/preference labels operationalize that concern. Historical
  human-inspector results do not establish language-model critic accuracy.
- **TD touch rules, target acquisition:** pp611–612 (PDF637–638) and the rendered
  equation at637 support attention to actual hit regions and nearby controls.
  Target acquisition is one part of total effort. Do not import a fixed motor
  constant, mouse-corner advantage or a whole-task time prediction into iPhone
  design. Current platform and relevant hardware/access checks remain necessary.
